The vote was won in steps: by the better-off in 1832,[^1832] by part of the urban working class in 1867,[^1867] by many country workers in 1884,[^1885] by women over 30 in 1918, and by all women, on the same terms as men, in 1928.[^equal]

## Reform, 1831–1832
The Act that passed in 1832 did away with the 56 smallest boroughs, gave two MPs each to 22 large towns, and gave the vote in the boroughs to men who paid a yearly rent of £10. The voters of England and Wales rose from about 400,000 to 650,000.[^1832a]

## The Chartists' MP, 1842–1847
The property qualifications of 1832 still kept most working men from the vote,[^1832b] and the Chartists wanted it for every man. Nottingham gave them the only MP they ever won.[^chartism]

## A vote for working men, 1865–1867
Before 1867 about one in seven of the adult men of England and Wales could vote. That year's Act gave the vote to part of the urban working class for the first time, and soon doubled their number.[^1867a]
- 1865: [[Samuel Morley]]

## Three seats, 1884–1885
The Act of 1884 carried the household vote into the counties, to men such as miners and farm labourers. The next year's Redistribution of Seats Act put most MPs in seats of one member, and divided large towns so that their suburbs were represented apart from their centres.[^1885a]
- [[Three seats for Nottingham, 1885]]

## Votes for women, 1895–1918
In 1897 Millicent Fawcett founded the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies, which pressed MPs by peaceful means. The Women's Social and Political Union, founded by the Pankhursts in 1903, turned militant; its members were the suffragettes.[^suff]
