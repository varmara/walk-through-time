The canal, the railways, the trams and the airfields each came and grew, and most of them went; some have come back.

## Canal and carriage, 1796–1919
The great age of canal building in England ran from the 1790s to the 1810s, much of it paid for by mine owners and merchants who had goods to ship.[^canal]

## The railway comes, 1832–1880
In the railway mania of the 1840s, 263 Acts of Parliament were passed to set up new railway companies in 1846 alone, and about a third of the lines they allowed were never built.[^mania]

## The last main line, 1899–1904
The Great Central's line to London, opened in 1899, was the last main line built in Britain until High Speed 1 in 2003. Most of it closed between 1966 and 1969.[^gc]

## Trams, buses and trolleybuses, 1900–1966
A company had run horse and steam trams in Nottingham from 1877; the Corporation's electric trams began on 1 January 1901.[^tram]

## Into the air, 1916–1930
King George V founded the Royal Flying Corps on 13 April 1912. By the end of March 1918 it had some 150 squadrons, and on 1 April it was joined with the navy's air service as the Royal Air Force.[^rfc]

## Closures, 1947–1972
In 1963 the Beeching report picked out 2,363 stations and 5,000 miles of line for closure, and most of them closed as planned.[^beeching]
- [[Gedling station]]

## Coming back, 1980s–2017
Britain's first modern street-running tramway opened in Manchester in 1992; until then Blackpool's, of 1885, was the only one of the first trams still running.[^metro] A few of the railways and stations closed in the 1960s have reopened too.[^reopen]
