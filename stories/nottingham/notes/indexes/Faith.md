Nottingham's churches and chapels record a long religious history: the medieval parishes, a priory dissolved at the Reformation, and the growth of Nonconformity and Methodism in the industrial town. William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army, was born here.

## Before the churches, Roman times–c. 1000
Christianity came to the Anglo-Saxons with the Gregorian mission, which landed in Kent in 597. Mercia, the kingdom that took in Nottingham, turned to it after its pagan king Penda died in 655.[^convert]

## Domesday and the Norman churches, 1086–1200
After the Conquest of 1066 the Normans brought the Romanesque style, with its round arches, to England. They rebuilt every English cathedral and almost every monastic church, and built hundreds of new parish churches.[^norman]

## The medieval parishes, 1200s–1400s
English Gothic is read in three styles: Early English, from the late 12th to the late 13th century; Decorated, from the mid-13th to the late 14th; and Perpendicular, from the mid-14th century on.[^gothic]

## The Reformation, 1535–1539
When the Pope refused to end Henry VIII's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, Parliament abolished the Pope's authority in England and made the king supreme head of the Church of England. The break with Rome gave the Crown control of the Church, and then of its monasteries.[^reform]

## War and rebuilding, 1643–1779
After the Civil War and the Restoration, the Act of Uniformity of 1662 required every minister to be ordained by a bishop and to follow the Book of Common Prayer. Over 2,000 clergymen refused and were expelled from their churches: the Great Ejection.[^unif]

## Chapels and Methodism, 1690–1839
The Toleration Act received royal assent on 24 May 1689. It let Protestant dissenters, such as Baptists, Congregationalists and Presbyterians, worship in their own meeting houses, once these were registered and their preachers licensed. It did not apply to Roman Catholics.[^toler]

## Churches for new districts, 1812–1903
Parliament's Church Building Act of 1818 gave £1 million for new churches, mainly in growing industrial towns where people had outrun the churches, and a second grant of £500,000 followed in 1824.[^cba]

## Missions and the Salvation Army, 1829–1909
On one Sunday in 1851 the census counted everyone at worship across England and Wales: 10,896,066 attendances, out of a population of 17,927,609. Fewer than half were at the Church of England, and the count was never repeated.[^census]
