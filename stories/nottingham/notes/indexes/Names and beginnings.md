Before anything here was written down, people left their names on the land and their things in the ground.

## Before the English, c. 1000 BC–AD 400
The Roman road called the Fosse Way was built in the first and second centuries AD, from Exeter in the south-west to Lincoln in the north-east, and crossed this county with a stopping place at Castle Hill near Bingham.[^fosse]

## The English settle, 6th–10th centuries
Most places in England still bear Old English names, and many of them hold the names of the settlers who founded them.[^oe] Nottingham is one: Snotingaham, the homestead of the Snotingas, the people of a man called Snot.[^snot]

## The Danes, 868–924
From 877 Danish armies settled the East Midlands, each round a fortified town: Derby, Leicester, Lincoln, Nottingham and Stamford, the Five Boroughs.[^five] They left names of their own, such as -by for a village and -thorpe for an outlying farm,[^norse] and in Nottingham the streets called -gate, from the Old Norse gata, a road: Barker Gate, Castle Gate, Fisher Gate.[^gata]

## Domesday Book, 1086
In 1085 the king sent his agents to survey every shire in England, to record what was owed to him and who now held the land. Its name came from the Last Judgement: from its record, as from that, there was no appeal.[^dom]

## Names given since, 1285–1807
After the Norman Conquest some places gained a second part to their name, the name of the family that now held them,[^manor] but England's place-names have changed little since the early Norman period.[^stable] The new names went to streets, districts and springs.
