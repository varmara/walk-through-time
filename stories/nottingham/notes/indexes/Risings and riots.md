For a century and more, Nottingham's people took to the streets again and again. The town lived by hosiery, settled there after 1730, and later by lace,[^hist] trades in which work was notoriously precarious.[^dom] Few of those workers had a vote: before 1832 only about one Briton in twenty did.[^vote] And the town grew so fast and so badly that it came to have the worst slums in England.[^hist] Each chapter here is one outbreak, set against what lay behind it.

## The cheese riot, 1766
Food riots were a familiar part of 18th-century life, with at least twelve major outbreaks. In 1766 poor harvests had already pushed up the price of grain, and when the first large-scale exports of it began that August, riots broke out across England; eight people were shot dead putting them down.[^food] Nottingham's came at its October fair.

## The Luddites, 1811
Stocking-knitters had taken organised action at times since 1675. The years 1810 to 1812 were hard ones, with high unemployment and inflation, made worse by the cost of the wars with Napoleon. The frame-breakers took their name from Ned Ludd, a probably mythical apprentice said to have smashed two stocking frames in 1779, and wrote as Captain, General or King Ludd.[^ludd]

## The Pentrich rising, 1817
Peace with France in 1815 brought no relief. Industrial change, demobilised soldiers and Corn Laws that kept bread dear drove the country into a deep depression. Radicals at Pentrich had their own Hampden Club, and the Home Office watched them through local magistrates and paid informers. The Pentrich men hoped to join a march on London in support of Sir Francis Burdett's bill for parliamentary reform.[^pent]

## The Reform riots, 1831
Parliament's seats had not been redrawn for population in many decades: some boroughs had a handful of voters, while Manchester had no MP at all. A bill to reform it passed the Commons in 1831, and its defeat in the Lords set off full-scale riots in Bristol and Derby as well as Nottingham.[^reform]

## The Chartists, 1842
The People's Charter of 1838 asked for six reforms, among them a vote for every man over 21, the secret ballot and pay for MPs. The Commons refused to hear its first petition, of 1.3 million signatures, in 1839, and rejected the second, of over three million, in May 1842.[^charter] That summer, after employers had cut wages by up to a quarter in the slump, a general strike spread from Stalybridge and Ashton to Manchester and beyond.[^strike]

## The Bogs, 1872
Enclosure turned land held in common into private property.[^encl] Nottingham's burgesses, who held common rights in the town's fields, had held off their enclosure from 1787 until 1845, though it kept the town from growing outwards.[^fields] Who made the attempt on Bulwell's Bogs is unclear: Samuel Thomas Cooper, lord of the manor since he bought Bulwell Hall, had died the year before, in 1871.[^cooper]
- [[The Bogs]]

## The St Ann's riots, 1958
St Ann's had been built after 1845 on the town's old Clay Fields, for the working poor, in terraces around courts; most of its houses had no inside toilet.[^stann] After the war people came from the Caribbean to work, in growing numbers from 1955, and met racism; by 1958 about one in ten of St Ann's people was not white.[^riot]
