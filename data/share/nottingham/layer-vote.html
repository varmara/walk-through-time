Reform and the vote

The vote was won in steps: by the better-off in 1832, by part of the urban working class in 1867, by many country workers in 1884, by women over 30 in 1918, and by all women, on the same terms as men, in 1928.

Reform, 1831–1832

The Act that passed in 1832 did away with the 56 smallest boroughs, gave two MPs each to 22 large towns, and gave the vote in the boroughs to men who paid a yearly rent of £10. The voters of England and Wales rose from about 400,000 to 650,000.

The castle burned, 1831

1674–79

When the House of Lords threw out the Second Reform Bill on 8 October 1831, rioting broke out in Nottingham and a crowd burned the castle, which belonged to the Duke of Newcastle, an opponent of reform. Three men were hanged; the Duke refused to restore the castle, and its burnt shell stood on the rock for years.

The Chartists' MP, 1842–1847

The property qualifications of 1832 still kept most working men from the vote, and the Chartists wanted it for every man. Nottingham gave them the only MP they ever won.

Sturge and Walter, 1842

1842

When George Larpent resigned his Nottingham seat, Joseph Sturge stood at the by-election: a reformer whose Complete Suffrage Union, a more middle-class rival to the Chartists' own association, campaigned for votes for all men. He lost, and the Conservative John Walter was elected on 4 August 1842; Walter was unseated on petition the next year, and the Liberal Thomas Gisborne won the seat in April 1843. Five years later Nottingham elected the Chartist leader Feargus O'Connor. Where the poll was held is not known.

The Battle of Mapperley Hills, 1842

1842

In August 1842 a meeting of more than two thousand in the Market Place voted to stop work until the People's Charter became law. The magistrates banned further meetings, and for days troops and police broke up gatherings across the town. On 23 August Chartists from Hucknall, Bulwell, Arnold, Basford and other places met on the common on Mapperley Hills; the 2nd Dragoon Guards were sent, and when the crowd, sitting on the grass to eat, would not disperse, Colonel Rolleston had about four hundred arrested. As they were marched into the town, a crowd stoned the soldiers and police. About fifty went for trial, some getting hard labour. Afterwards the town's workers turned instead against the Corn Laws and the price of bread.

Feargus O'Connor

1847 · Statue of Feargus O'Connor

Feargus Edward O'Connor was an Irish Chartist leader, MP and advocate of the Land Plan, which sought to provide smallholdings for the labouring classes. A highly charismatic figure, O'Connor was admired for his energy and oratory, but was criticised for alleged egotism. His newspaper Northern Star was widely read among workers, becoming the voice of the Chartist movement.

Wikipedia: Feargus O'Connor

Feargus O'Connor, the leader of Chartism and owner of its newspaper, the Northern Star, was elected MP for Nottingham in 1847, defeating Thomas Benjamin Hobhouse. His statue by J. B. Robinson was put up in the Arboretum in 1859.

A vote for working men, 1865–1867

Before 1867 about one in seven of the adult men of England and Wales could vote. That year's Act gave the vote to part of the urban working class for the first time, and soon doubled their number.

Samuel Morley, was an English woollen manufacturer and political radical. He is known as a philanthropist, Congregationalist dissenter, abolitionist, and statesman.

Three seats, 1884–1885

The Act of 1884 carried the household vote into the counties, to men such as miners and farm labourers. The next year's Redistribution of Seats Act put most MPs in seats of one member, and divided large towns so that their suburbs were represented apart from their centres.

Three seats for Nottingham, 1885

1885

From 1283 the borough of Nottingham sent two members to Parliament. In the mid-18th century the great landowners, the Duke of Newcastle for the Whigs and Lord Middleton for the Tories, held sway, and the town usually returned one of each. In 1885 the old two-member seat was abolished, and the city was divided into three seats of one member each: Nottingham East, South and West.

Votes for women, 1895–1918

In 1897 Millicent Fawcett founded the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies, which pressed MPs by peaceful means. The Women's Social and Political Union, founded by the Pankhursts in 1903, turned militant; its members were the suffragettes.

Helena Dowson

1895 · Nottingham Magistrates' Court

Helena Dowson ran Nottingham's suffrage society from 1895 and its shop on Long Row. In 1920 she became the first woman magistrate and a Liberal councillor for the Meadows; a plaque here at the Justice Centre remembers her.

Votes for women, 1918

1918

The Representation of the People Act, passed on 6 February 1918, gave the vote to women of 30 and over who met a property qualification, and they voted for the first time on 14 December 1918. That November women had also been allowed to stand for Parliament: in Nottinghamshire the first to do so was Violet Markham, at Mansfield, who lost. In 1919 Bridgid Burke-Bloor became the first woman to stand for Nottingham City Council, and in 1920 Helena Dowson and Caroline Harper were its first women councillors. Women won the vote on the same terms as men on 2 July 1928. A hundred years to the day after that first vote, the city unveiled a plaque by a juniper tree in the Arboretum for the suffragette Helen Watts.
