Bulwell

Bulwell, recorded in Domesday Book, was a market town on the River Leen with its own trades: limestone quarrying, glove-making and bleaching. Its people defended their common land, the Bogs, when the lord of the manor tried to enclose it in 1872.

Buleuuelle

1086

Domesday Book, in 1086, records Bulwell as the village of Buleuuelle. The name probably comes from a spring, the Bull Well, or from an Anglo-Saxon man called Bulla and his well.

Bulwell market

c. 1320

A local law of about 1320 said that only those with covered premises could trade, so Bulwell's market grew up with stalls that were covered while in use and opened out when not. The Market Place has kept much of its old shape.

Bulwell stone

16th century

Bulwell's limestone was quarried for building. Stretches of the wall round Wollaton Hall, built in the late 16th century, are of Bulwell stone.

Bulwell Hall

1770

The landowner John Newton built Bulwell Hall in 1770. It was sold at auction in 1864 to Samuel Thomas Cooper, and was pulled down in 1956; its grounds are now Bulwell Hall Park.

Gloves and bleach

c. 1840

Victorian Bulwell lived by the Leen. Glove-making, begun in about 1840, employed some 700 people until 1864, and by 1853 there were three bleachworks along the river.

St Mary's, Bulwell

1849

Storms in 1843 damaged the old church of St Mary beyond repair, and a new one was built on its site in 1849–50 to the designs of Henry Isaac Stevens. It is listed Grade II.

A Luddite shot at Bulwell, 1811

1811

On the night of 10 November 1811 some 20 to 30 frame-breakers walked from Arnold to Bulwell Forest, met about 40 more, and marched on the house of the hosier Edward Hollingworth, who had barricaded it and armed his friends. Shots were exchanged in the dark; the Luddites drove the defenders out through a back window and smashed all seven frames, with the doors, windows and furniture. At dawn the body of one of the attackers lay by the road at Bulwell Forest: John Westley, a framework knitter who had lived in Arnold for twelve years. The inquest found the killing justifiable, in self-defence. As far as is known, he was the only man killed in the Nottinghamshire disturbances.

The Bogs

1872

The Bogs, the wet meadow by the Leen, were common land. When the lord of the manor tried to enclose them in 1872, about a thousand people marched on the land to claim it, peacefully: after a short march they sat down to lunch by the river. The Corporation later bought the land for the town. A bigger play-park was built in 2003, and the park won a Green Flag Award in 2004.
