North of the centre

North of the old town lay open fields and common land until the 19th century. As Nottingham expanded, the area was used for public parks and cemeteries, a Catholic cathedral, a college and a great railway station. It is now part of the city centre.

Nottingham General Hospital

1782

A legacy from the banker John Key paid for a hospital on part of Nottingham Park. Its first building, designed by John Simpson, was begun on 12 February 1781 and opened with 44 beds in September 1782; John Wesley was an early visitor. In 1844 it took in the hurt from a hanging on High Pavement, where twelve died on Garner's Hill: Wikipedia says a stand collapsed, but the newspapers of the day describe the crowd being forced down the steps into Garner's Hill and trampled. Hine added a storey in 1855, and Alfred Waterhouse's round Jubilee Wing opened in 1900. It closed in 1992, its work moved to the Queen's Medical Centre.

The Arboretum war memorial

1862

The Arboretum's bell tower is a war memorial to the 59th (2nd Nottinghamshire) Regiment of Foot, designed by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton in 1862–63. Its cupola held a bell that British troops took from a temple in Canton during the Second Opium War, until the bell went to the regimental museum in 1956. Two of the cannon beside it were taken at Sebastopol in the Crimean War; the other two are copies.

The Boer War memorial, 1903

1903

On 26 March 1903 General Lord Methuen unveiled a granite obelisk to the officers and men of the city and county of Nottingham who died in the South African War of 1899–1902. It carries all their names and their regiments, and it was given to the city by the lace manufacturer Thomas Isaac Birkin. It first stood at the bottom of King Street, and was moved here, to the corner of the Forest, to make way for trolleybuses.

Votes for women, 1918

1918

The Representation of the People Act, passed on 6 February 1918, gave the vote to women of 30 and over who met a property qualification, and they voted for the first time on 14 December 1918. That November women had also been allowed to stand for Parliament: in Nottinghamshire the first to do so was Violet Markham, at Mansfield, who lost. In 1919 Bridgid Burke-Bloor became the first woman to stand for Nottingham City Council, and in 1920 Helena Dowson and Caroline Harper were its first women councillors. Women won the vote on the same terms as men on 2 July 1928. A hundred years to the day after that first vote, the city unveiled a plaque by a juniper tree in the Arboretum for the suffragette Helen Watts.

Guildhall

1888

Built in 1887–88 to designs by Thomas Verity and George Henry Hunt, chosen in a competition judged by Alfred Waterhouse, it replaced the old Guild Hall on Weekday Cross and served as the city's magistrates' court. The council used it as offices until 2010; it has stood largely empty since.

Maid Marian, 2026

2026

On 17 July 2026 Alicja Biała's statue of Maid Marian was unveiled at the corner of Tollhouse Hill and Upper Parliament Street. It stands on a sandstone boulder, and is made from native plants, among them Nottingham catchfly, oak, birch, hawthorn and bluebell, cast in bronze from wax moulds. She invited more than a hundred local people to workshops, where they brought plants, some from their own gardens; the cast pieces were welded into one figure 2.3 m tall.

From inn to inn, 1755

1755

For more than a century Nottingham's Freemasons had no hall of their own. One lodge, meeting in the town from 1755, moved from inn to inn: the Red Lion on Climber Street by 1763, the Leg of Mutton on Sandy Lane, then three inns on Parliament Street in turn, the Kingstone Arms, the New George and the Milton's Head, and later the Crown and Cushion, the Poultry Hotel and the Castle and Falcon on Warser Gate. From 1844 it met in the Assembly Rooms on Low Pavement, and in 1881 it settled at the Masonic Hall on Goldsmith Street. Another lodge, named after the Milton's Head inn, whose records run from 1790, belonged to the rival Antients; its warrant was cancelled in 1799 and restored the next year.

The School of Art's stone, 1863

1863

Building on the new School of Art in Waverley Street began on 23 May 1863, but its foundation stone waited until 22 October, when the 5th Duke of Newcastle could come. He was then the Provincial Grand Master of Nottinghamshire's Freemasons, and he laid the stone with the usual Masonic ceremonies; the band played "God Save the Queen" and the crowd joined in. Thirty-five years later it was remembered as the last such ceremony the Masons had performed in Nottingham itself.

The Masonic Hall, Goldsmith Street

1880 · made 1928

When the Duke of St Albans became Nottinghamshire's Provincial Grand Master in 1878, the brethren raised the idea of a hall of their own. It was built here on Goldsmith Street and opened in 1880, as a meeting place for the lodges and a Masonic club. Lodges that had met in inns moved in from 1881. The present hall, of brick with stone details in the Neo-Classical style usual for Masonic halls, was designed in 1928 by John Howitt & Son, one of the last works of John Howitt, himself a Freemason; the city lists it as a local heritage asset. The first lodge consecrated in it was Temple Lodge, on 30 October 1930.
