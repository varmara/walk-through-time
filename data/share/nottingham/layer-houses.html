Clifton and Wilford

On the south bank of the Trent: Clifton Hall with its grove and dovecote, Wilford House and the manor of Wilford, and the West Bridgford estate said to have been won at cards.

Clifton Hall

13th century

The Clifton family held the manor of Clifton from the late 13th century, and this was their manor house. Sir Gervase Clifton largely rebuilt it between 1778 and 1797, partly to designs by John Carr of York. In 1958 the family sold it, ending 700 years of ownership; it became a girls' grammar school, and is now two houses. The hall itself dates from the late 16th century and is listed Grade I. In its grounds are a brick ice house of the mid-18th century, the vaulted remains of a curved garden stairway, a pair of Coade stone lions of the early 19th century, and the late 18th-century stable block, now cottages.

The manor of Wilford

1280

Before the Conquest the manor of Wilford belonged to Gode the Countess. In Domesday Book the settlement is named Willesforde, with a fishery, a priest and 23 sokemen. Clifton and Wilford, Peverel lands, passed to the Crown early in Henry II's reign, and the king granted them to Gerbode de Escalt; in Richard I's time they passed to the de Rodes family. Before 1280 Gervase de Clifton obtained both manors from Sir Gerard de Rodes for £30 a year, and a charter of 1280 confirmed the grant. In 1853 Sir J. G. J. Clifton was lord of the manor, owned all its land but the church's glebe, and was patron of the church; in 1870–72 the manor still belonged to Sir R. J. Clifton.

Won at cards

The Musters family gained the West Bridgford estate in 1523, reputedly by winning it in a game of cards. When they sold land for building they set strict rules for it, and the village grew into a suburb, with its own council from 1894 and part of the borough of Rushcliffe from 1972.

Clifton Grove

1677

A local account says Sir William Clifton planted the avenue of elms in 1677, from a little stone bridge over Fairham Brook up to the hall; it was said to be wide enough for six carriages abreast. In 1853 it was "an avenue of trees a mile in length". By the 18th century the Grove was a favourite outing, and an 1874 guide tells of thousands of Nottingham artisans there at Easter and Whitsuntide. The Nottingham poet Henry Kirke White, a butcher's son, published Clifton Grove, a Sketch in Verse, in 1803, aged 17. D. H. Lawrence set a scene of Sons and Lovers (1913) in the Grove.

Clifton dovecote

18th century

The red-brick dovecote on the green was built in the early 18th century for the Clifton family's farming estate. It is said to be one of the largest in England, with about 2,300 nesting places; pigeons were kept for winter meat. A stone tablet on its side remembers the men of the village who died in the First World War. It is listed Grade II.

Wilford House

1781

The house was designed in 1781 for the Smith family as Wilford Hall, the centre of an estate with stables, lodges for the house staff and a private chapel. It is a red-brick country house of three storeys and five bays, listed Grade II, and its stable block of the same year is listed too. The Smiths' bank in Nottingham is believed to have been the first bank outside London, and Samuel Smith, an MP for 44 years, is believed to have built the house; by 1790 it was his. The banker Abel Smith died at Wilford on 12 July 1788. His wife's younger sister, Elizabeth Bird, was the mother of William Wilberforce, the slavery abolitionist, who stayed at the house for several months between 1786 and 1789. In 1853 it was the seat of Henry Smith, a large, handsome brick mansion; he owned about 70 acres of the parish, and in 1828 he built an infant school in the village. Thomas Forman, who founded the Nottingham Post, later bought the house from the Smiths, and it passed to the Forman Hardy family; at some point it was renamed Wilford House. It became offices in the 1980s, the Wilford Place estate was built on its grounds in 2006, and in 2021 the house was given a multi-million pound refurbishment.

Wikipedia: Wilford

Thrumpton Hall

1617

The Powdrells, a Roman Catholic family, had a house here, which they lost after the Babington Plot. Around it the Pigots built a rose-brick house, largely complete by 1617. Part of the Powdrells' house survives inside it, with a priest hole.

The staircase

1617

The Pigot family built Thrumpton Hall in rose-coloured brick, and it was largely complete by 1617. In the 1660s Gervase Pigot altered and improved it. Its Jacobean cantilevered staircase was carved from the estate's own timber, with John Webb, a pupil of Inigo Jones, overseeing the work.

The Byrons of Thrumpton

1607–17

The hall came to the Byrons when George Byron, 8th Baron Byron, married Lucy Wescomb in 1843, and they kept it for about a hundred years. Frederick, the 10th Baron, was the village's vicar and from 1917 its squire as well: its "squarson".

Ruddington Hall

1860

In 1860 Thomas Cross, a banker from Bolton, built the hall. From 1880 it was the home of Philo Laos Mills, an American merchant who became High Sheriff of Nottinghamshire. In the Second World War it became a hospital, and it stayed one until 1980.

East of the city

East of the city: Holme Pierrepont Hall and the Pierreponts, Tollerton Hall, which became a seminary, and Shelford House, stormed in the Civil War.

The Pierreponts

1257

Pierrepont is Norman French for "stone bridge". In 1257 Sir Henry Pierrepont married Annora de Manvers, heir to Holme, and the family stayed for almost seven centuries: Charles I made Sir Robert Pierrepont Earl of Kingston-upon-Hull in 1628, and George I made Evelyn Pierrepont a duke in 1715. The estate was broken up and sold in 1940.

Wikipedia: Holme Pierrepont

Holme Pierrepont Hall

c. 1280

The Pierreponts held Holme from about 1280. Around 1500 the hall was built, one of the few brick houses of its time in Nottinghamshire. The family became Earls and Dukes of Kingston, then Earls Manvers, and the hall is listed Grade I.

The storming of Shelford House

In November 1645 Parliament's troops under Sydnam Poyntz, with Colonel John Hutchinson leading the assault, stormed Shelford House, held for the King by Philip Stanhope. About 160 of its defenders were killed, and the house was burnt that night.

Tollerton Hall

1948

In 1948 the Roman Catholic Diocese of Nottingham opened St Hugh's College at Tollerton Hall, a junior seminary for boys from 11. By 1969 it was taking boys who were not training for the priesthood, and it closed in 1986. The hall was then a company's headquarters until 2017, when it was bought as a private home again. It is listed Grade II.

Wikipedia: Tollerton, Nottinghamshire

The city and the Park

In and just outside the city: the dukes' mansion on the castle rock, the Park estate that its residents now run, and the hall and grange of Mapperley and Woodthorpe.

Nottingham Castle

1670s · 1068

The first castle on the rock was a wooden motte-and-bailey, begun in 1068 on William the Conqueror's orders, and rebuilt in stone under Henry II. For centuries it was a royal fortress and an occasional royal residence. After the Civil War it was razed in 1651, and in the 1670s the 1st Duke of Newcastle began a mansion on the rock. Rioters burnt the mansion in 1831; it reopened as a museum in 1878.

Mapperley Hall and the Wrights

1790s

In the 1790s Ichabod Wright built Mapperley Hall at the heart of his estate. His grandson, Colonel Charles Ichabod Wright, began selling land for houses in 1873, but most of the estate was released only in 1903; some 163 houses were approved in Mapperley Park between 1906 and 1914.

Hine's Park

1854

In 1854 the Duke's estate took on the Nottingham architect Thomas Chambers Hine, to design many of the Park's villas. The roads are still lit by gas lamps, believed to be one of the largest such networks left in Europe.

Woodthorpe Grange

1874

Henry Ashwell, a Basford bleacher, built Woodthorpe Grange in 1874. The council bought the house and grounds in 1921 for £15,000, Jesse Boot giving £5,000 of it, and the park opened in 1922.

Sold, saved and run by its residents

1938

The 8th Duke of Newcastle sold the Park in 1938 to the Nuffield Trust, which passed it on to Oxford University. The estate became a conservation area in 1969, and talks that began in 1986 between the residents' association and the university put the estate in the hands of its residents, who run it from the Estate Office.

North and west

North and west of the city: Wollaton Hall, whose restoration Cassandra Willoughby oversaw, Bestwood Lodge, Papplewick Hall and Bulwell Hall.

Wollaton Hall

1580

Sir Francis Willoughby built Wollaton Hall between 1580 and 1588, probably to designs by the Elizabethan architect Robert Smythson. After a fire in 1642 the house stood empty for some forty years; Wyatville remodelled it from 1801. The 11th Baron Middleton sold it to Nottingham Corporation for £200,000, and since it opened to the public in 1926 it has held the city's natural history museum.

Wikipedia: Wollaton Hall

Bestwood Lodge

1862

The present lodge was built in 1862–65 for the 10th Duke of St Albans by Samuel Sanders Teulon, in the Gothic Revival style. The army took it over in the Second World War, and in the mid-1970s it became a hotel. It is listed Grade II*.
