Castle and Crown

After the Norman Conquest William the Conqueror built a castle at Nottingham and gave it into the keeping of William Peverel. For six centuries it was the Crown's; then it was the dukes'; at last it was the town's.

The royal castle, 1068–1600

William also made Sherwood a royal hunting forest, under forest laws, covering perhaps a quarter of the county; King John and Edward I hunted there. The castle was the kings' house beside it.

Statue of Robin Hood

13th century · made 1952

Robin Hood is the legendary outlaw of English folklore, a skilled archer said to rob the rich to give to the poor. The earliest ballads are usually thought to be set in the 13th or 14th century, and the first clear mention of Robin Hood rhymes comes in Piers Plowman, in the 1370s. In the stories he lives in Sherwood Forest, and his enemy is the Sheriff of Nottingham. James Woodford's bronze of him at Nottingham Castle was unveiled in 1952 and listed Grade II in 2018.

1194

While Richard I was away, the castle was held by supporters of his brother, Prince John, among them the Sheriff of Nottingham. In March 1194 King Richard came and besieged it, building siege machines like those he had used on crusade.

The Welsh hostages, 1212

1212

King John took hostages in numbers no king had before. In 1212, when the Welsh rose against him, he came to Nottingham with his army and, the chronicler Roger of Wendover says, before he either ate or drank had twenty-eight youths hanged, whom he had received from the Welsh as hostages the year before. The Magna Carta Project puts them at some thirty, and calls it notorious.

A royal hunting park

1286

The first written record of a royal hunting lodge at Bestwood is from 1286, in the reign of Edward I. Charles II and Nell Gwyn visited it, and in 1682 he leased the lodge to her. Their son, Charles Beauclerk, Earl of Burford, was made Duke of St Albans in 1684, and Bestwood passed down through his descendants.

Mortimer's Hole

1330

In 1330 Roger Mortimer, 1st Earl of March, ruled England with Queen Isabella as regents for her young son, Edward III. That October Sir William Montagu led Edward's men up a secret tunnel through the castle rock to a door high in the castle, and they seized Mortimer; he was sent to the Tower of London and hanged a month later. The long passage to the bottom of the rock is known as Mortimer's Hole.

London's mayor at the castle, 1392

1392

After a riot in Fleet Street, Richard II summoned the mayor, sheriffs and aldermen of London to appear before him and his council at Nottingham Castle on 25 June 1392. The mayor and sheriffs were removed from their offices and imprisoned, and a royal warden was put in charge of the City: London lost its self-government until it had paid the king to win it back.

Edward IV's tower, 1476

1476

After Henry IV's queen, Joan of Navarre, lived here until 1437, the castle was little kept up, until the Wars of the Roses made it a stronghold again. Edward IV proclaimed himself king in Nottingham, and in 1476 ordered a new tower and royal apartments, which John Leland, writing in 1540, thought the finest part of the castle. On 20 August 1485 Richard III rode from Nottingham to Leicester, two days before he was killed at Bosworth.

The castle in decay

1525

Henry VIII sent new tapestries ahead of his visit in August 1511, but a survey of 1525 found the castle in "dekay and ruyne", part of the Great Hall's roof fallen in. It was garrisoned again in 1536, against the Pilgrimage of Grace, and in 1562 it was to host a meeting of Elizabeth I and Mary, Queen of Scots, which never took place. By 1600 it was no longer a royal home.

The Civil War, 1642–1651

The war grew out of a long struggle between the monarchy and Parliament for political and religious control. When the fighting began in August 1642, Charles left York for Nottingham, close to the Royalist Midlands.

Henry Ireton

1642 · 1611

Henry Ireton, eldest son of German Ireton of Attenborough, was baptised in St Mary's Church here on 3 November 1611. He fought for Parliament at Edgehill in October 1642. On the night before Naseby, in June 1645, he surprised the Royalist army; the next day he was wounded and captured, and Cromwell later freed him. He married Bridget, Cromwell's eldest daughter, was one of the commissioners who signed the death warrant of Charles I, and died of disease outside Limerick on 26 November 1651.

John Hutchinson

1643 · St Margaret's Church

Colonel John Hutchinson was an English politician and army officer who played a significant role during the English Civil War. He was a Member of the House of Commons of England from 1646 to 1653 and briefly in 1660. Hutchinson was a prominent Puritan leader and served in the parliamentary army.

Wikipedia: John Hutchinson (Roundhead)

John Hutchinson, born at Owthorpe in 1615, held Nottingham Castle for Parliament from 1643 and signed Charles I's death warrant. He died a prisoner in 1664 and is buried at Owthorpe; his wife Lucy's Memoirs of his life made him famous.

Hutchinson's garrison

1643

In 1643 Colonel John Hutchinson took command of Nottingham Castle, and Parliament later made him governor of both town and castle. It was a hard post: the town had no walls, the garrison was small and short of supplies, and the committee that ran the town quarrelled. Hutchinson held on all the same, and protected the radical separatists among his soldiers as far as he could.

St Nicholas' Church

1643 · 1678

A church of St Nicholas stood here from the 11th or 12th century. In the Civil War, Royalists took its tower and fired on the castle's garrison, so in 1643 the castle's governor, Colonel John Hutchinson, had it destroyed. For 28 years there was no church here; the brick church standing today was begun in 1671 and finished in 1678.

Royalist raids on Nottingham

1644

The Royalists of Newark raided again and again. Sir Charles Lucas broke into the town in January 1644 and tried to set it on fire, and in April 1645 a party from Newark took the fort at Trent Bridge. Each time Hutchinson won back what was lost, and each demand that he surrender the castle he answered with fresh defiance.

Lucy Hutchinson

Owthorpe

Lucy Hutchinson was an English translator, poet, and biographer, and the first person to translate the complete text of Lucretius's De rerum natura into English verse, during the years of the Interregnum.

Wikipedia: Lucy Hutchinson

Lucy Hutchinson, born in the Tower of London in 1620, was the first to translate the whole of Lucretius into English verse, and her Order and Disorder is perhaps the first epic poem by a woman in English. Her life of her husband, John Hutchinson, written for her family, was printed in 1806. She died at Owthorpe in 1681.

The castle's deer park

1663

The Park began as a wooded deer park on the west side of Nottingham Castle, hunted while the castle belonged to the Crown. In 1663 William Cavendish, 1st Duke of Newcastle, bought the ruined castle together with its park, and the land stayed with his successors for almost three centuries.

Nottingham Castle

1670s · 1068

The first castle on the rock was a wooden motte-and-bailey, begun in 1068 on William the Conqueror's orders, and rebuilt in stone under Henry II. For centuries it was a royal fortress and an occasional royal residence. After the Civil War it was razed in 1651, and in the 1670s the 1st Duke of Newcastle began a mansion on the rock. Rioters burnt the mansion in 1831; it reopened as a museum in 1878.

Princess Anne at Nottingham, 1688

1688

In the Glorious Revolution of 1688, James II's daughter Princess Anne turned against her father. She and her friend Sarah Churchill slipped out of Whitehall by a back staircase into the care of Bishop Compton, and reached Nottingham on 1 December. Here she and her party stayed at the Feathers Inn, lodged at the charge of the Earl of Devonshire.

The castle burned, 1831

1674–79

When the House of Lords threw out the Second Reform Bill on 8 October 1831, rioting broke out in Nottingham and a crowd burned the castle, which belonged to the Duke of Newcastle, an opponent of reform. Three men were hanged; the Duke refused to restore the castle, and its burnt shell stood on the rock for years.

Nottingham Castle Museum

1878

The burnt mansion stood a ruin until Thomas Chambers Hine restored it in 1875. The Prince of Wales opened it in 1878 as Nottingham Castle Museum, the first municipal art gallery in the United Kingdom outside London. The castle closed in 2018 for a redevelopment that cost £30 million, and reopened on 21 June 2021 under the charity Nottingham Castle Trust. In November 2022 the trust said it was appointing liquidators, and the site went back to the council.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Castle
