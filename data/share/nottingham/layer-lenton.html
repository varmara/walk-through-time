Lenton

Lenton was dominated for over four centuries by its Cluniac priory, founded in about 1102 and dissolved in 1538. Industrial growth came in the 19th century, with Raleigh's bicycle works, and in the 20th the University of Nottingham was built at Highfields, where MRI was later developed.

Lenton Priory

1538

William Peverel founded a priory of Cluniac monks at Lenton in about 1102–08, and richly endowed it. In February 1538 its last prior, Nicholas Heath, and many of his monks were thrown into prison, accused of high treason. The prior, eight monks and four Lenton labourers were executed, in Nottingham and in front of the priory, and the priory was dissolved that year. Little is left: the Priory Church of St Anthony is thought to keep part of its hospital chapel.

St Mary de la Roche

12th century

A chapel was cut into the sandstone here, about a quarter of a mile west of the castle, and from at least the late 12th century two monks from Lenton kept it. In 1474 it passed to the King and Gervase Clifton in exchange for the chapel at Tickhill Castle. Puritans wrecked much of it in the Civil War, calling the caves "Papist Holes", and in the mid-19th century they were made into refreshment rooms. The site is now behind flats on Castle Boulevard.

Keighton

1276

The medieval village of Keighton stood on the hillside that is now part of University Park. It belonged to Lenton Priory and is first mentioned in 1276; by 1387 its last five houses were in ruins. Kilns here later made roof and floor tiles, probably for the priory. The site was found in 1940, when the field was dug for allotments, and excavated from the 1950s.

Lenton Fair

1584

Henry II gave Lenton Priory an eight-day fair for St Martin's Day, which Henry III lengthened to twelve, with tolls paid to the monks for every booth. The fair outlived the priory: in 1584, when servants of Mary, Queen of Scots came, it was a horse fair, and by the 17th century it was known as a great fair for all sorts of horses. In the 19th century farmers and horse dealers still came.

Trent Building

1928

On 10 July 1928 King George V and Queen Mary opened a building of Portland stone designed by Morley Horder. It now belongs to the University of Nottingham and is listed Grade II.

Saturday Night and Sunday Morning

1958

Alan Sillitoe's 1958 novel became a 1960 film directed by Karel Reisz, with Albert Finney as Arthur Seaton, who works at the Raleigh factory. Much of the exterior location filming took place in Nottingham, and shooting at a Nottingham factory ran two days over schedule and £3,500 over budget.

The MRI scanner

Peter Mansfield came to the University of Nottingham in 1964 and developed magnetic resonance imaging here. Just before Christmas 1978 a full-body scanner was installed, and he made the first scan of a live patient. He shared the Nobel Prize in 2003 with Paul Lauterbur.

Queen's Medical Centre

1977

Queen Elizabeth II opened the Queen's Medical Centre on 28 July 1977, and it took its first patient the next year. It was the first hospital in the UK built to be a teaching hospital, with the University of Nottingham's medical and nursing schools on the same site, and with more than 1,300 beds it was long the largest hospital in the country.

A brain scanner you can wear, 2018

2018

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) maps the brain at work from the tiny magnetic fields its cells make, but its scanners weighed about half a tonne, because their sensors had to be kept at −269 °C, and the patient had to keep still. In March 2018 researchers at the Sir Peter Mansfield Imaging Centre, with UCL, showed in Nature a scanner worn like a helmet, built from new "quantum" sensors in a 3D-printed shell. Wearing it, people could nod, stretch, drink tea and even play ping pong while their brains were scanned.
