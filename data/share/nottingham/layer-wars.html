Soldiers and wars

Nottingham sent soldiers and sailors to wars across the centuries and raised its own county regiment. The two World Wars also reached the city itself, through the munitions factories and depots, the losses at Chilwell and the bombing of 1941.

A French marshal on parole, 1705–1711

The War of the Spanish Succession set Britain and its allies against France. At Blenheim on 13 August 1704 the Duke of Marlborough and Eugene of Savoy destroyed a French and Bavarian army; more than 14,000 French soldiers were taken prisoner, among them their commander.

Marshal Tallard at Newdigate House, 1705

1705

Newdigate House on Castle Gate, built around 1675 for Thomas Charlton the younger, of Chilwell, was where the French commander Marshal Tallard was kept from 1705 to 1711 after his defeat at Blenheim. He lived here on parole. Daniel Defoe counted his small formal garden, laid out in the French fashion, among the beauties of Nottingham, and Tallard is credited with bringing celery into English cooking. He went home to France on his release in 1711. From 1960 the house was the United Services Club.

At sea, 1710–1822

Admiral Sir John Borlase Warren, born at Stapleford, sat as MP for Nottingham from 1797 while still at sea. In October 1798 his squadron, off Donegal, did much to defeat a French fleet sent to invade Ireland. He died in 1822, and has a monument in St Mary's Church, Attenborough.

Captain John Deane

1710

In 1710 Captain John Deane's ship, the Nottingham Galley, sank off Boon Island, New England. He later served in the Russian navy under Peter the Great, and in 1736 he retired to Wilford. He is buried in St Wilfrid's churchyard.

The county regiments, 1779–1908

From 1782 regiments of foot were given county names, and Nottinghamshire had two: the 45th, and the 59th, raised in 1755, which became the 2nd Nottinghamshire. The 59th fought in China in the Second Opium War and in Afghanistan in 1878–1880. Under the Childers reforms of 1881 it joined the East Lancashire Regiment.

The Sherwood Foresters

1779

A regiment raised in 1741 became, at the request of the people of Nottinghamshire, the 45th (1st Nottinghamshire) Regiment of Foot in 1779. Against Napoleon it fought through the Peninsular War from 1808 to 1814, and at Talavera in 1809 won the nickname "Old Stubborns". Renamed the Sherwood Foresters in 1866, it was joined with the Derbyshire regiment on 1 July 1881 as the Sherwood Foresters (Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire Regiment), whose museum is in the castle.

The Arboretum war memorial

1862

The Arboretum's bell tower is a war memorial to the 59th (2nd Nottinghamshire) Regiment of Foot, designed by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton in 1862–63. Its cupola held a bell that British troops took from a temple in Canton during the Second Opium War, until the bell went to the regimental museum in 1956. Two of the cannon beside it were taken at Sebastopol in the Crimean War; the other two are copies.

The Boer War memorial, 1903

1903

On 26 March 1903 General Lord Methuen unveiled a granite obelisk to the officers and men of the city and county of Nottingham who died in the South African War of 1899–1902. It carries all their names and their regiments, and it was given to the city by the lace manufacturer Thomas Isaac Birkin. It first stood at the bottom of King Street, and was moved here, to the corner of the Forest, to make way for trolleybuses.

War work, 1914–1918

In 1915 a shortage of artillery shells at the front became a political crisis, and Lloyd George was made Minister of Munitions, at the head of a new government department. New factories were built to fill shells, Chilwell's among them.

Louisa May Long

1914 · Florence Boot Hall

Florence Boot, born in St Helier, Jersey, in 1863, married Jesse Boot in 1886 and helped him run the firm. She brought books and stationery into the Goose Gate shop and started Boots Book-Lovers' Library and cafés in the stores. She founded the university's first hall for women, named Florence Boot Hall after her.

Wikipedia: Florence Boot

National Shell Filling Factory

Viscount Chetwynd built the factory from 1915 to fill shells with explosive; it filled some 19 million during the First World War. On 1 July 1918 eight tons of TNT exploded, killing 134 people, or 137 by another count. The site is now Chetwynd Barracks.

Albert Ball

1917 · Plaque to Albert Ball

Albert Ball, was a British fighter pilot during the First World War. At the time of his death he was the United Kingdom's leading flying ace, with 44 victories, and remained its fourth-highest scorer behind Edward Mannock, James McCudden and George McElroy. Born and raised in Nottingham, Ball joined the Sherwood Foresters at the outbreak of the First World War and was commissioned as a second lieutenant in October 1914.

Remembering, 1921–1927

After the war thousands of memorials went up across Britain and its empire. The national one, the Cenotaph in Whitehall, was unveiled by George V on 11 November 1920, and many towns modelled theirs on it.

Getting ready, 1939–1942

Just before the Second World War the RAF built a system of air defence: radar reports went to a filter room, which sifted them into single plots before fighters were sent up. Its fighters were divided into groups, and 12 Group, covering the Midlands, was based at Hucknall and later at Watnall.

Ruddington Depot

1940 · 1899

Ruddington's station on the Great Central Railway opened on 15 March 1899. From 1940 a depot was built here to fill and store ammunition, and it closed in 1983. The station had closed in 1963, and the Nottingham Heritage Railway now runs trains on the line. The rest of the depot's land is a business park and Rushcliffe Country Park.

The filter room at Watnall

1940 · 1943

In spring 1940 Watnall became the headquarters of No. 12 Group of Fighter Command, which covered the Midlands, Norfolk, Lincolnshire and North Wales. In a bunker here, the filter room sifted reports of enemy aircraft before they went to the operations room further up the road. It opened in 1943 and served as an early warning centre until 1961, and in 2008 it was sold to be made into a hotel.

Wikipedia: RAF Watnall

Tollerton airfield

1940s

Licensed in 1929 and opened in 1930 by Sir Sefton Brancker, it was one of Britain's first municipal aerodromes. In the Second World War it repaired Lancaster bombers; its 18 concrete pillboxes are all listed. The airfield closed on 6 June 2025, sold to the Vistry Group to build 4,000 houses.

Chilwell's ordnance depot

1942

Soon after the First World War the shell factory's site became a depot and barracks for the Royal Army Ordnance Corps. A film shot on 9 August 1942 shows Churchill and Matilda tanks for Russia being loaded onto a train here. It was still a central ordnance depot after the Second World War. Its central vehicle kit store moved to Bicester in 1958, but the site went on as a general stores depot and vehicle workshop.

Bombs and losses, 1941–1943

The Blitz, the German bombing of British cities, ran from 7 September 1940 to 16 May 1941; more than 40,000 civilians were killed by bombing during the war. Nottingham's worst night came in its last fortnight.

The Nottingham Blitz, 1941

built in 1377

On the night of 8–9 May 1941 the Luftwaffe bombed Nottingham. Firefighters saved St Mary's Church after an incendiary burned through its roof; three Boots factories, in Poplar Street, Island Street and Station Street, were on fire; University College and the Shakespeare Street chapel were hit. The worst loss was at the Co-op bakery on Meadow Lane, where 49 people died. In all, 159 people were killed that night.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Blitz

Vera Anderson

1941 · Capital One

Vera Anderson, a Boots nurse, was on duty when bombs wrecked the Boots Printing Works here on 8 May 1941. She gave first aid to a dying night watchman through burning debris, then treated casualties at the control centre, and was awarded the George Medal.
