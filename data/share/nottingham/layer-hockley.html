Hockley

Hockley lies below the Lace Market, where Nottingham's cloth workers gave the medieval street Walker Gate its name. In the 18th century Arkwright built his first cotton mill here, Methodists opened a chapel, and in 1849 John Boot opened his shop on Goose Gate. More recently it has become known for its independent cinemas.

Walker Gate

1285

From about 1285 the area was known as Walker Gate, after the "walking", or treading, of cloth to thicken it. Nottingham's wool trade mattered: between 1343 and 1345 the price of wool in its market was the standard for all of England.

Arkwright's first mill

Richard Arkwright's first cotton mill opened in Nottingham in 1768, its machines turned by horses. By 1772 it had four storeys and 300 workers.

Broadway

The building began in 1839 as a grand Wesleyan chapel on Broad Street. Broadway opened here in 1990, and one of its screens is the only cinema screen designed by Sir Paul Smith.

Boots on Goose Gate

1849

John Boot opened a herbalist's shop on Goose Gate in 1849, and his son Jesse was born in the area, into poverty, the next year. After John died in 1860 his widow Mary kept the shop going as M & J Boot. Jesse became a partner at 21, and in 1883 he built a larger store on the same site, the first of the Boots shops; Nottingham Civic Society put up a plaque here in 2015.

Pulp Fiction and the Screen Room

2002

Hockley has a taste for small cinema. Broadway was the first cinema in the UK to show Quentin Tarantino's Pulp Fiction, and from 2002 to 2011 the district also had The Screen Room, then the smallest commercial cinema in the world.

The trolleybuses

1927

Trolleybuses, buses drawing power from overhead wires, first ran on 10 April 1927 and took over the tram routes one by one; the last tram ran in September 1936. A depot opened on Parliament Street in June 1929 and is still in use. By the end of 1937 the city had 125 trolleybuses, the largest fleet in the country, and at most 157. The last ran on 30 June 1966.
