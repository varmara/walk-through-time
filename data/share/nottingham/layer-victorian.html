The Victorian town

In the 19th century Nottingham broke out of its old bounds. In 1877 the borough took in the parishes of Basford, Bulwell, Radford, Sneinton and others round it.

A town hemmed in, 1790s–1840s

Pressure to enclose the common fields round the town for building began in 1787, but the burgesses who had rights in them, and the owners of town sites, who gained as land grew dearer, held it off until 1845. Building could spread only to a few places, such as Radford, Hyson Green, Sneinton and Carrington, and the framework knitters stayed in the most crowded parts of the town.

Mapperley Hall and the Wrights

1790s

In the 1790s Ichabod Wright built Mapperley Hall at the heart of his estate. His grandson, Colonel Charles Ichabod Wright, began selling land for houses in 1873, but most of the estate was released only in 1903; some 163 houses were approved in Mapperley Park between 1906 and 1914.

Samuel Hall's Sherwood

1825

Mansfield Road was the main road from London to Yorkshire, and a turnpike from 1787. Samuel Hall laid out Sherwood's first streets beside it in 1825; the road was paved and lit in 1879, the year horse trams arrived.

Tower House is one of the earliest houses in the Park, built to P. F. Robinson's designs between 1827 and 1833. Watson Fothergill later gave it the tower that names it. It is listed Grade II.

Park Terrace

1829

The first house in the park, built in 1809 opposite the castle gatehouse, was the vicarage of St Mary's. In 1827 the architect Peter Frederick Robinson published a plan for building over the park, and houses began to go up along Park Terrace in about 1829; within three years there were 40 to 50 of them.

St Ann's Allotments

1830s

In the 1830s the Clay Fields were divided into plots, and the gardens on Hunger Hill became the St Ann's Allotments, the oldest and largest allotment site in England. Hungerhill Gardens alone has 456 plots over about 52 acres, with sheds and summerhouses from the 19th century; the whole site is listed Grade II*.

The body snatchers of Barker Gate, 1827

1827

As St Mary's churchyard filled, the parish opened burial grounds on Barker Gate, the first in 1742 on land bought from the Duke of Kingston for ten shillings, known as Middle Bury, and another in 1786, Top Bury. In 1827 "resurrection men" tried to send two bodies to London for the anatomists; as families checked the recent graves, it was found that thirty bodies had been stolen. The two found were an old woman, Dorothy Townsend, and Mrs Rose's three-year-old son. A man called Davies, thought to be one of the gang, barely escaped with his life when crowds mobbed him in Nottingham and Arnold, and the two were buried again.

General Cemetery

1836

In 1836 a cemetery of 18 acres was established at the top of Sion Hill, now Canning Circus, with a gatehouse and almshouses by Samuel Sutton Rawlinson. More than 150,000 people are buried here.

A railway suburb

1840s

The textile trade and the railways grew Carlton in the 19th century: its population climbed steadily from the 1840s, and by 1850 it had become a suburb with its own houses, churches and schools. The tram line from Nottingham reached it in 1914.

The fields enclosed, 1845–1856

The Inclosure Act of 1845 opened the fields to building, and made room too for a chain of green spaces, among them the Arboretum and the Forest.

New Town

1845

Until the Inclosure Act of 1845 the area was open Clay Fields. The Act let the town take 1,068 acres for housing, and by 1880 the streets of terraced houses called New Town were complete. The terraces north of Victoria Park are listed, as a once common kind of house now mostly lost to bombs and clearance.

The Arboretum

1852

On 11 May 1852 a park laid out under the botanist Samuel Curtis opened here. Its bandstand went up in 1907. The bell from a temple in Canton, taken in the Second Opium War, hung in its war memorial until 1956.

Hine's Park

1854

In 1854 the Duke's estate took on the Nottingham architect Thomas Chambers Hine, to design many of the Park's villas. The roads are still lit by gas lamps, believed to be one of the largest such networks left in Europe.

Who lived in the Park

The Park's villas housed the town's business and public figures: the publisher Richard Allen, who lived at Albert Villa until his death in 1884; the MP Anthony Mundella, for whom Amelia House was built; and Captain Athelstan Popkess, chief constable of Nottingham from 1930 to 1959, at 35 Newcastle Drive.

Adams Building

1855

Built from 1854 to designs by Thomas Chambers Hine for Thomas Adams, a Quaker lace manufacturer, and opened in 1855 as his lace showroom and warehouse, with a chapel for the workers in the basement. Inspectors thought it a model example of its kind; since 1999 it has been part of Nottingham College.

Building the lace town, 1865–1898

From 1870 a school board was set up wherever school places fell short, to give children of 5 to 12 an elementary education, though it was not yet free or compulsory.

Theatre Royal

1865

Built for the lace manufacturers John and William Lambert, who wanted a prestigious theatre for the town, to designs by Charles J. Phipps; its portico of six Corinthian columns closes the view up Market Street. It opened on 25 September 1865 with Sheridan's The School for Scandal, and in 1897 Frank Matcham remodelled its auditorium. In October 1952 it staged the world premiere of Agatha Christie's The Mousetrap.

Wikipedia: Theatre Royal, Nottingham

Clifton village school

1871

Henry Robert Clifton laid the school's cornerstone on 3 November 1871, and it opened in April 1872 with 48 children; a night school for men began a year later. It closed in 1956, when new schools were built on the estate, and is now the village hall.

Malt Cross

1877

The site was once an inn called the Roebuck. In 1877 a music hall was built here, to designs credited to Edwin Hill, under a high arched glass roof on laminated wooden arches. It took its name from a monument that once stood in the market square, and it is one of the few Victorian music halls to survive.

University College

Built in 1877–81 for University College Nottingham, with the town's library and a natural history museum, and damaged by bombs in the Second World War. D. H. Lawrence studied here to become a teacher; it is now Nottingham Trent University's Arkwright Building.

Guildhall

1888

Built in 1887–88 to designs by Thomas Verity and George Henry Hunt, chosen in a competition judged by Alfred Waterhouse, it replaced the old Guild Hall on Weekday Cross and served as the city's magistrates' court. The council used it as offices until 2010; it has stood largely empty since.

Watson Fothergill's offices

1895

Watson Fothergill, born Fothergill Watson, took his mother's family name in 1892 and built his own offices here, on George Street, in 1895. Its front has a statue of a medieval architect and busts of Augustus Pugin and George Edmund Street; it is listed Grade II.

Furley & Co

1896

Watson Fothergill designed this shop and warehouse for Furley & Co, provision merchants, in 1896–97; Lloyds Bank has it now. Terracotta panels show the sugar trade, from cutting and boiling the cane to boats and a far eastern market, and the Clinton Street side is faced with glazed Art Nouveau tiles.

Royal Concert Hall

1898 · made 1982

In 1898 Frank Matcham designed and built the Empire Palace of Varieties here. It closed for the last time in 1958 and was demolished for road-widening in 1969. On 27 November 1982 the Royal Concert Hall, designed by the Renton Howard Wood Levin Partnership, opened on the site, with Elton John as its first performer.

A city, 1889–1900

In 1889 Nottingham became a county borough, under the Local Government Act of 1888.

Village to suburb

1891

Wilford parish had 2,769 people in 1891 and about 4,500 by 1901. South Wilford became part of West Bridgford in 1935, and in 1952 Wilford became part of the City of Nottingham. The Silverdale estate was built on its farmland in the late 1950s, and Compton Acres in 1986.

Radford grows

1891

Factories, offices and warehouses filled Radford: Raleigh, once the world's largest bicycle maker, and Player's cigarettes among them. By 1891 the parish had 38,718 people, and on 1 April 1897 it was joined to Nottingham.

Nottingham Victoria station

built in 1899

Nottingham Victoria, the Great Central and Great Northern railways' station, opened on 24 May 1900 and closed on 4 September 1967. It was demolished except for its clock tower, which still stands at the entrance of the Victoria Centre built on the site.
