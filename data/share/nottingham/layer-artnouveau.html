Art Nouveau

Art Nouveau, the style of curving lines and plant forms that was at its height around 1900, never shaped Nottingham's large buildings, which stayed Gothic or Baroque. It arrived in the details instead: in shopfronts and the tiles of a shop's side wall, in a station's iron gates, and in the porches of ordinary terraced houses.

Shops and a station, 1896–1904

Art Nouveau was most popular between 1890 and 1910, and drew on the curves of plants and flowers. In Britain it was also called the Modern Style, and London's Liberty store did much to spread it through its floral fabrics.

Furley & Co

1896

Watson Fothergill designed this shop and warehouse for Furley & Co, provision merchants, in 1896–97; Lloyds Bank has it now. Terracotta panels show the sugar trade, from cutting and boiling the cane to boats and a far eastern market, and the Clinton Street side is faced with glazed Art Nouveau tiles.

Albert Nelson Bromley

1902 · A building

Albert Nelson Bromley was an English architect based in Nottingham.

Wikipedia: Albert Nelson Bromley

The architect Albert Nelson Bromley designed these houses on Newcastle Drive in 1878, and died here in 1934. His buildings in the city include Boots' stores on Pelham Street and Turney Street. He designed many of Boots' shops, in Nottingham and in other towns, and in 1904 the young Thomas Cecil Howitt, later the architect of the Council House, joined his office as an apprentice.

Boots on Pelham Street

1902

Boots' central store was built on the corner of Pelham Street and High Street to designs by Albert Nelson Bromley: 1902–05 by Historic England's dates, 1903–04 in Wikipedia's list of his works. Its shopfront, with curving Art Nouveau panels, has hardly changed and is a rare survivor of its kind. Historic England suggests it may be the first Art Nouveau shopfront in the country, and that Harrods inspired it. Boots left in 1972; Zara trades here now.

Nottingham station

1904

Nottingham's first station, opened in 1839 by the Midland Counties Railway, stood across Carrington Street; a through station replaced it on this site in 1848. The present building, of red brick and terracotta, was designed by the local architect Albert Edward Lambert and opened on 16 January 1904. Its carriage entrances have Art Nouveau gates of wrought iron. A pair taken down in 1951, made in Leicester in 1904, is now in the Science Museum Group's collection.

Lace money in Long Eaton, 1901–1906

The railways brought Long Eaton its growth, and lace mills went up, Harrington Mill of 1885 among them. By 1907 the town had almost 1,400 lace machines and over 4,000 people, a quarter of its population, worked in lace.

Art Nouveau Long Eaton

1901

The town's new wealth shows in its Art Nouveau buildings: York Chambers of 1901 and the Midland Counties District Bank of 1903, at 38 and 40 Market Place, and the Carnegie Library on Tamworth Road of 1906, by the same architects, Gorman and Ross. York Chambers and the library are listed Grade II.

On the doorstep, 1900–1914

Sneinton's population rose from 17,439 in 1891 to its peak of 23,093 in 1901. Land was sold to developers, and the houses they built on the slopes of Sneinton Dale were of a higher standard than the streets before them.

Sneinton's porch tiles

1900

Most of the terraced houses in more than thirty Sneinton streets were built between 1900 and 1914, and many have glazed tiles in their front porches: over 250 designs have been recorded. Loughborough Avenue has water-lilies, blackberries and Art Nouveau flowers like stained glass, Holborn Avenue stylised flowers, and Port Arthur Road flowers in blues, yellows and oranges. Since the mid-1980s more than an eighth of them have gone: stripped for sale, painted over or hidden behind new doors. The pins are approximate.
