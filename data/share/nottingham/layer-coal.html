Coal

Britain's coal output peaked in 1913, at 287 million tonnes; its last deep mines closed in 2015. Nottinghamshire's pits, and the wagonways, canals, railways and power stations that served them, lie between those dates.

Wagons and canals, 1603–1839

The great age of canal building in England ran from the 1790s to the 1810s. The first canals were paid for by merchants, makers and mine owners who needed to ship their goods.

Wollaton Wagonway

In 1603–1604 Huntingdon Beaumont and Sir Percival Willoughby laid a wooden wagonway from the Strelley coal pits to Wollaton Lane End, its wagons drawn by horses. It is described as the first railway in Britain.

The Nottingham Canal

built in 1895

Authorised in 1792 and engineered by William Jessop, the canal opened in 1796 from the Trent to Langley Mill, bringing coal to the town. Railways took its trade; most of the main line was abandoned in 1937.

The railway that began in a pub

In 1832 coal owners meeting at the Sun Inn, Eastwood, proposed extending the Mansfield and Pinxton Railway to Leicester. It became the Midland Counties Railway, whose line from Nottingham, at Carrington Street, to Derby opened in 1839.

Deep pits and ironworks, 1861–1899

Coal employed more people in Britain than ever before or since in 1920, when 1,191,000 worked in it.

Hucknall's pits

1861

Hucknall had been a village of framework knitters. In 1861 the Hucknall Colliery Company sank its Top Pit off Watnall Road, and in 1866 its Bottom Pit off Portland Road, and in under a century Hucknall grew into a market town. The Top Pit closed in 1943 and the Bottom Pit in 1986. A mining memorial in the town centre names both pits.

Sir Robert Clifton's colliery

Coal was found on the Clifton estate, and work on Clifton Colliery began in 1868. Sir Robert Juckes Clifton, the last of the Clifton baronets, died of typhoid in 1869; the colliery and this toll bridge, both his projects, opened in 1870.

Bennerley Ironworks

c. 1870

E. P. Davis built the Bennerley furnaces, near Ilkeston, in about 1870; by 1880 they belonged to the Awsworth Coal and Iron Company, which was putting up a new furnace. A branch of the viaduct's railway served the works. They made their last pig iron in March 1931, and in 1934 Thos. W. Ward of Sheffield bought the three furnaces to take them down. Bestwood winding engine

1873

The Bestwood Coal and Iron Company's colliery here wound up its coal and lowered its miners with a vertical steam engine of 1873. The pit closed in 1967, but the winding engine house and its engine survive.

Coal and iron at Bestwood

1875

The Lancaster family sank Bestwood's coal mine in 1875. Beside it the Bestwood Coal and Iron Company opened an ironworks in 1881 with two blast furnaces, added two more in 1890, and kept it working until 1928. The company built the terraces of Park Road, St Albans Road and The Square for its workers, and its offices, with their clock tower, on Park Road.

Bennerley Viaduct

1877

In 1876–77 a viaduct was built here of wrought iron, light enough for ground undermined by coal workings. It closed in 1968 but was too costly to take down, and in 2022 it reopened to walkers and cyclists. The only other viaduct like it is Meldon Viaduct in Devon.

Gedling Colliery

1899

Gedling Colliery opened in 1899 and was the area's main employer for almost a century. In the 1960s it dug over a million tonnes of coal a year with 1,400 men, about one in ten of them from the Caribbean, and was known as the "pit of all nations"; 128 men died there over its life. It closed in 1991, and in 2015 its grounds opened as Gedling Country Park.

Wikipedia: Gedling

Coal for power, 1925–2024

From 1926 a Central Electricity Board built Britain's first national grid, finished in September 1933. Later the biggest power stations were built on the coalfields themselves, such as West Burton, in the Nottinghamshire coalfield near the Trent.

Wilford Power Station

1925

In 1925 a coal-fired power station opened on the north bank of the Trent. It closed in 1981 and was soon demolished, and the Riverside Retail Park stands on its site.

The power station that was not built

1960

In 1960 the Central Electricity Generating Board proposed a 2,000 MW power station at Holme Pierrepont, with coal by rail and cooling water from the Trent. A public inquiry of 14 days turned it down, partly because it would have shut off the sand and gravel beneath. The gravel was dug instead, and in 1971 the pits became the National Watersports Centre.

Ratcliffe-on-Soar Power Station

1968

In 1968 a coal-fired power station with eight cooling towers began generating here. It became the last coal-fired power station in Britain, and when it shut in September 2024 it ended 142 years of coal power in the country.

The last pits, 1947–1999

On 1 January 1947 most mines passed to the new National Coal Board, and in the 1950s and 1960s closing pits became routine. After the strike of 1984–85 the Nottinghamshire miners split from the National Union of Mineworkers and formed a union of their own, the Union of Democratic Mineworkers, in December 1985.

Calverton Colliery

1952

Calverton Colliery was officially opened on 24 September 1952, and the village grew from 1,304 people in 1951 to 5,658 in 1961. British Coal shut the mine on 19 November 1993; it worked again under RJB Mining until it closed for good in April 1999.

Wikipedia: Calverton, Nottinghamshire

Cotgrave Colliery

The colliery came in the early 1960s: from 1962 hundreds of mining families moved here from Radford's closed pit, and the village grew several times over. The pit worked through the 1984–85 strike and closed in 1993. Its pithead site is now the Hollygate Park estate, and the spoil heap became Cotgrave Country Park. The pin is approximate.

Woodside Colliery

1960s

Woodside was one of the Shipley pits. Nationalised in 1947, it closed with Coppice in the 1960s as uneconomic, ending more than 250 years of deep mining at Shipley. The Shipley Lakeside development now covers its site.

Wollaton Colliery

1965

Coal has always mattered in Wollaton, from the wagonway onwards. Wollaton Colliery closed in 1965, and houses now cover its ground and that of the old canal: the Torvill and Dean estate.
