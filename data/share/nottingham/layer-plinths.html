Statues and memorials

Statues and memorials show who a town chose to honour, and when. Nottingham's range from a Chartist leader and Victorian MPs to the city's war dead and, in the 20th and 21st centuries, figures of legend and football, and at last the women of its past.

Victorian heroes, 1859–1905

Nottingham's first public park, the Arboretum, opened on 11 May 1852 on land set aside under the town's Inclosure Act of 1845. It became the town's first home for statues: a Chartist MP and the memorial of a county regiment.

Statue of Feargus O'Connor

1859

The stone statue of Feargus O'Connor, by J. B. Robinson of Derby, went up in the Arboretum in 1859: the Chartist leader whom Nottingham had elected its MP in 1847. It is listed Grade II.

The Arboretum war memorial

1862

The Arboretum's bell tower is a war memorial to the 59th (2nd Nottinghamshire) Regiment of Foot, designed by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton in 1862–63. Its cupola held a bell that British troops took from a temple in Canton during the Second Opium War, until the bell went to the regimental museum in 1956. Two of the cannon beside it were taken at Sebastopol in the Crimean War; the other two are copies.

Statue of Robert Juckes Clifton

1883

Sir Robert Juckes Clifton, of the Clifton family across the river, was elected MP for Nottingham in 1861 and died of typhoid in 1869, aged 42. The Wilford Toll Bridge he had planned opened the next year, and in 1883 William Philip Smith's stone statue of him was put up at its northern end. It is listed Grade II.

The Boer War memorial, 1903

1903

On 26 March 1903 General Lord Methuen unveiled a granite obelisk to the officers and men of the city and county of Nottingham who died in the South African War of 1899–1902. It carries all their names and their regiments, and it was given to the city by the lace manufacturer Thomas Isaac Birkin. It first stood at the bottom of King Street, and was moved here, to the corner of the Forest, to make way for trolleybuses.

Statue of Queen Victoria

1905

Albert Toft's stone statue of Queen Victoria was unveiled in the Market Square in 1905. In 1953 it was moved to the Memorial Gardens on Victoria Embankment, and in 2022 it was cleaned and repaired, its sceptre mended and missing fingers replaced. It is listed Grade II.

Between the wars, 1921–1929

After the First World War thousands of memorials went up across Britain and its empire, many of them modelled on the Cenotaph in Whitehall, unveiled on 11 November 1920. In Nottingham the war dead came first; then a new Council House gave the Market Square its stone lions.

Statue of Captain Albert Ball

1917 · made 1921

Henry Poole's bronze of the airman Albert Ball, with an allegorical female figure, in the castle gardens. Ball was killed on 7 May 1917, and the memorial was unveiled in 1921; it is listed Grade I.

City War Memorial

1927

On 11 November 1927 a memorial designed by the City Engineer Thomas Wallis Gordon was unveiled on land beside the Trent given by Sir Jesse Boot. It remembers the men of Nottingham who died in both World Wars.

Memorial to Samuel Morley

1928

Joseph Else's memorial to Samuel Morley, of bronze and stone, stands at the Arboretum's Waverley Street entrance: the hosier who was elected the town's MP in 1865. The list of the city's public art dates it to 1928. It is listed Grade II.

Council House

1929

The Council House, designed by Thomas Cecil Howitt, opened in 1929 on the site of the old Exchange at the head of the Old Market Square. The sculptor Joseph Else named the two stone lions on its steps Agamemnon and Menelaus. Little John, the hour bell in its dome, weighs over 10 tonnes, and its strike can be heard 7 miles away.

Legend and football, 1952–2008

After the Second World War the city stopped raising statues to its MPs and soldiers, and turned to the figures it was known for around the world: a legendary outlaw and a football manager.

Statue of Robin Hood

13th century · made 1952

Robin Hood is the legendary outlaw of English folklore, a skilled archer said to rob the rich to give to the poor. The earliest ballads are usually thought to be set in the 13th or 14th century, and the first clear mention of Robin Hood rhymes comes in Piers Plowman, in the 1370s. In the stories he lives in Sherwood Forest, and his enemy is the Sheriff of Nottingham. James Woodford's bronze of him at Nottingham Castle was unveiled in 1952 and listed Grade II in 2018.

Statue of Brian Clough

1975 · made 2008

Brian Clough managed Nottingham Forest from January 1975 to May 1993. Under him the club won the League in 1977–78 and the European Cup in 1979 and 1980. Les Johnson's statue of him, at the corner of King Street and Queen Street, was unveiled by his widow Barbara on 6 November 2008.

Honouring women, 2019–2026

Fewer than one in twenty of Britain's sculptures show a woman who was not royal.

Florence Paton's sculpture, 2019

2019

On International Women's Day, 8 March 2019, a stainless steel bust of Florence Paton, Nottinghamshire's first woman MP, was unveiled in the Garden for the Blind on Carlton Hill. She sat for Rushcliffe, which then took in Carlton, from 1945 to 1950, and pressed for safer mines and better health care for women and children. The sculptor Hilary Cartmel gave it miners' picks and a lamp for her ties to the area, books for her work in education, and the crowned portcullis of Parliament. The garden itself, of herbs and scented plants in raised beds, was made for people with little or no sight.

Standing in This Place, 2025

2025

On 6 February 2025 a life-size bronze by the sculptor Rachel Carter and the community history group the Legacy Makers was unveiled in the Green Heart at Broad Marsh. It shows two women in the dress of their time: a white woman from an East Midlands textile mill, and an enslaved Black woman from the cotton fields of America. It was paid for by donations of more than £250,000, and the Art Fund helped the National Justice Museum, which looks after it, to acquire it.

Maid Marian, 2026

2026

On 17 July 2026 Alicja Biała's statue of Maid Marian was unveiled at the corner of Tollhouse Hill and Upper Parliament Street. It stands on a sandstone boulder, and is made from native plants, among them Nottingham catchfly, oak, birch, hawthorn and bluebell, cast in bronze from wax moulds. She invited more than a hundred local people to workshops, where they brought plants, some from their own gardens; the cast pieces were welded into one figure 2.3 m tall.
