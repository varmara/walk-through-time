Markets and fairs

After the Normans, Nottingham was two boroughs, the English one round the old settlement and the French one under the castle. The open ground between them was built on as the town grew, and became the Old Market Square. Edward I's charter, approved on 12 February 1284, gave the town its first mayor, with two bailiffs who kept the customs of each borough.

Charters, markets and fairs, 11th–14th centuries

After the Norman Conquest the right to hold a market was the king's to grant. A charter let the lord take tolls, and no new market could be set up within about a day's travel of an old one.

Old Market Square

11th century

One of the largest paved squares in the United Kingdom. The two towns were once divided by a wall across it, whose line a stainless steel channel now marks; Goose Fair was held here until 1928.

Lenton Fair

1584

Henry II gave Lenton Priory an eight-day fair for St Martin's Day, which Henry III lengthened to twelve, with tolls paid to the monks for every booth. The fair outlived the priory: in 1584, when servants of Mary, Queen of Scots came, it was a horse fair, and by the 17th century it was known as a great fair for all sorts of horses. In the 19th century farmers and horse dealers still came.

Ilkeston Charter Fair

1252

Henry III granted Ilkeston its charter in 1252, and its fair is held in the Market Place. Two fairs were joined into one in 1888, and the Charter Fair has been held every October since.

Goose Fair

1284

A royal charter of 1284 refers to Nottingham's fairs. For centuries Goose Fair was held in the Old Market Square, until in 1928, having outgrown the city centre, it moved to the Forest Recreation Ground. It was cancelled for the Great Plague in 1646 and during the World Wars.

Walker Gate

1285

From about 1285 the area was known as Walker Gate, after the "walking", or treading, of cloth to thicken it. Nottingham's wool trade mattered: between 1343 and 1345 the price of wool in its market was the standard for all of England.

Bulwell market

c. 1320

A local law of about 1320 said that only those with covered premises could trade, so Bulwell's market grew up with stalls that were covered while in use and opened out when not. The Market Place has kept much of its old shape.

Cross and Exchange, 1529–1726

In 1449 the town became a county corporate, governing itself.

Weekday Cross

1529

Before the Old Market Square, Weekday Cross, by the Guild Hall, was the town's main market and its centre. A cross went up here in about 1529–30, and by 1711 people called it "the Pillar". Butter, eggs, pigeons, wild fowl, fruit and fish were sold on Wednesdays and Fridays. The Corporation had the cross pulled down and sold in 1804; Nottingham Civic Society put up a new one in 1993. The Guild Hall's site is now Nottingham Contemporary's.

The Exchange

1724

In 1724–26 the town's Exchange was built in the Market Place to a design by the mayor, Marmaduke Pennell. It held the Corporation's offices, and its Great Hall was used for concerts, elections and balls. It was demolished in 1926 to make way for the Council House.

The cheese riot, 1766

Food riots were a familiar part of 18th-century life. In 1766 poor harvests had pushed up the price of grain, and when it began to be shipped abroad that August, riots broke out across England.

The cheese riot, 1766

1766

After a poor harvest in 1766, food was dear and people feared shortages. At Goose Fair that October, when Lincolnshire merchants began carting away cheese they had bought, the townspeople tried to stop them; the fair turned to a riot, shops, a warehouse and a boat were looted, and wheels of cheese were rolled down the streets. The mayor could not restore order, and soldiers fired on the crowd, killing William Eggleston, a farmer guarding his own goods. It took several days to bring the town back to order.

From market to shop, 1849–1972

Shops of one name in many towns, the chains, began in the railway age: W. H. Smith opened its first station bookstalls in 1848.

Boots on Goose Gate

1849

John Boot opened a herbalist's shop on Goose Gate in 1849, and his son Jesse was born in the area, into poverty, the next year. After John died in 1860 his widow Mary kept the shop going as M & J Boot. Jesse became a partner at 21, and in 1883 he built a larger store on the same site, the first of the Boots shops; Nottingham Civic Society put up a plaque here in 2015.

Wikipedia: Hockley, Nottingham

1964
