Knitting

The county's cloth trades began with wool and the stocking frame, invented at Calverton in 1589, and went on to cotton mills, bleachworks, Luddite frame-breakers and silk.

Walker Gate

1285

From about 1285 the area was known as Walker Gate, after the "walking", or treading, of cloth to thicken it. Nottingham's wool trade mattered: between 1343 and 1345 the price of wool in its market was the standard for all of England.

The stocking frame

1589

In 1589 William Lee of Calverton invented the stocking frame, a machine that knitted. Legend has it that Elizabeth I refused him a patent. The trade grew here, and from 1728 Nottingham was one of its main centres.

Wikipedia: Stocking frame

The frame-knitting town

1728

After William Lee died in about 1614 the stocking frame spread slowly: in 1644 London had some 400 to 500 frames and Nottinghamshire about 150. In 1728 Nottingham's magistrates refused the authority of the London Company of Framework Knitters, and the town became a chief centre of the trade. England had some 14,000 frames by 1750, and in 1758 Jedediah Strutt's "Derby rib" let them knit ribbing. Short of yarn, the knitters looked for ways to spin cotton here.

Arkwright's first mill

Richard Arkwright's first cotton mill opened in Nottingham in 1768, its machines turned by horses. By 1772 it had four storeys and 300 workers.

The Leen cotton mills

1778

In 1778 George, James and John Robinson took a 56-year lease from Frederick Montagu to build cotton mills on the Leen. With Castle Mill, Grange Farm, Middle Mill and Forge Mill, and the dam and Moor Pond that fed them, they employed around 800 people, many of them apprentices brought from the St Marylebone workhouse in London. In 1785 James Robinson bought a Boulton and Watt engine for the mill at Bestwood, said to be the first rotative steam engine in a cotton mill anywhere. Spinning had stopped by 1828, and most of the buildings were taken down in the 1850s.

papplewick.org: The cotton mills on the Leen (Papplewick Parish Council)

Arnot Hill House

1791

The mill owner John Hawksley built a large cotton mill here, working before the end of 1791 with a 60-horsepower engine, and laid out Arnot Hill Park in about 1792 as a setting for his house. His son Thomas Hawksley, born at Arnot Hill House in 1807, became the engineer who gave Nottingham clean water. The house is listed Grade II.

Wikipedia: Arnold, Nottinghamshire

The first Luddites

The Luddite movement began in Arnold on 11 March 1811. Across Nottinghamshire the frame-breakers went after the "wide" knitting frames used to make cheap goods.

Framework Knitters' Museum

c. 1829

In about 1829 cottages were built for Ruddington's framework knitters, who worked at hand frames in workshops behind them; the workshops date from about 1840. All are listed, and together they now make up the Framework Knitters' Museum.

Lowe's Silk Mill

During the Reform riots of 1831, Lowe's Silk Mill in Beeston was burnt on 11 October, the day after Nottingham Castle, and the same day the riots ceased.

Gloves and bleach

c. 1840

Victorian Bulwell lived by the Leen. Glove-making, begun in about 1840, employed some 700 people until 1864, and by 1853 there were three bleachworks along the river.

The bleachers of Basford

1851

The Leen's clean water brought the bleachers. A directory of 1853 counted eight bleaching works in Basford, with many stocking frames and bobbin-net machines, and a population that had grown from 2,124 to 10,093 by 1851.

Lace

Lace made Nottingham's fortune in the 19th century, from the Leavers machine and Thomas Adams's warehouse to the big mills of Long Eaton.

The Leavers lace machine

John Leavers lived here, at Canning Circus, where a plaque calls him the inventor of the Leavers lace machine, in 1813. He improved John Heathcoat's lace machine, and in 1821 emigrated to Grand-Couronne in Normandy.

Adams Building

1855

Built from 1854 to designs by Thomas Chambers Hine for Thomas Adams, a Quaker lace manufacturer, and opened in 1855 as his lace showroom and warehouse, with a chapel for the workers in the basement. Inspectors thought it a model example of its kind; since 1999 it has been part of Nottingham College.

Wikipedia: Adams Building, Nottingham

Long Eaton lace

1907

Lace made Long Eaton. High Street Mill dates from 1857, West End Mill of 1882 stands beside the Erewash Canal, and by 1907 the town had almost 1,400 lace machines and more than 4,000 people in the trade.

Wikipedia: Long Eaton

High Pavement Chapel

1982 · 1690 · made 1876

The congregation went back to 1690 and became Unitarian in 1802. In 1876 it built this chapel, to designs by Stuart Colman. Worship ended in 1982; the chapel then housed a lace museum, and it is now a Pitcher & Piano.

Nottingham Contemporary

2009

Garners Hill, said to be the oldest site in Nottingham, once held cave dwellings, a Saxon fort and a medieval town hall, until the Victorians cleared it for a railway line. On 14 November 2009 an art centre designed by Caruso St John opened here, its walls embossed with a Nottingham lace pattern.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Contemporary
