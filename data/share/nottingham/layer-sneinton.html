Sneinton

Sneinton lies on the hill east of the city. Its windmill belonged to the family of the mathematician George Green, William Booth was born here in 1829, and the city's wholesale market moved here in the 1930s. Cave dwellings were once dug into its sandstone ridge.

Green's Windmill

c. 1807

Shortly after 1807 the baker George Green built a tower mill here. It burned in 1947, was restored and reopened in 1986, and is now part of a science centre.

William Booth's birthplace

c. 1820

William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army, was born in Sneinton on 10 April 1829. At 13 he was apprenticed to a pawnbroker. His birthplace is now a museum.

Penicillin at Daleside Road, 1944

1944

In the Second World War the Ministry of Supply asked Boots to design and run a plant that would make penicillin in quantity, by growing the mould on the surface of its broth. Boots built it on Daleside Road, and made huge amounts of penicillin there during the war. It was the largest surface-culture plant in the country, or by another account in the world, and one of the first penicillin factories built anywhere. Its monthly production reports run from August 1944. In 1945 a newly qualified pharmacist, Stewart Adams, later the leader of the team that found ibuprofen, took his first job there.

Ibuprofen

Ibuprofen was first made at Boots in Nottingham in December 1961, by Stewart Adams's team. Their later laboratory, on Pennyfoot Street, is now BioCity, and in 2013 a Royal Society of Chemistry blue plaque marked the discovery. Adams and the chemist John Nicholson filed the patent in 1961; the drug was first sold in Britain in 1969, as Brufen, and in 1983 it became the first such painkiller apart from aspirin sold over the counter in the UK.

Sneinton Hermitage

1904

Cave dwellings dug into the sandstone ridge extended out into Sneinton, where they were known as the Hermitage. When the road below was made, houses with brick fronts were built against the rock, the caves serving as their back rooms, among them two pubs, the White Swan and the Earl Manvers' Arms. On 10 May 1829 an overhanging rock fell, crushing two houses and the dancing room cut in the rock. In 1888 the London and North Western Railway opened a goods station on top of the rock, lowering it by 25 feet and pulling down the rock houses in the way of its bridge. In 1897 the Great Northern's high-level line pushed the road some 60 feet north, and most of the houses and caves to the west went; in 1904 those east of the railway bridge were blasted or pulled down. What little remains can still be seen along Sneinton Hermitage.

Sneinton's porch tiles

Most of the terraced houses in more than thirty Sneinton streets were built between 1900 and 1914, and many have glazed tiles in their front porches: over 250 designs have been recorded. Loughborough Avenue has water-lilies, blackberries and Art Nouveau flowers like stained glass, Holborn Avenue stylised flowers, and Port Arthur Road flowers in blues, yellows and oranges. Since the mid-1980s more than an eighth of them have gone: stripped for sale, painted over or hidden behind new doors. The pins are approximate.
