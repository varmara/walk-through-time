The town centre

Buildings, streets and a station that the middle of the town has lost, most of them demolished in the 20th century.

Broad Marsh

1539

This was boggy ground on the edge of the medieval town, where the Franciscan friary of Greyfriars stood until it was closed in 1539. After large-scale slum clearance, the Broad Marsh shopping centre was officially opened on 25 March 1975. It closed in 2020 and was left partially demolished; caves found beneath it became part of the City of Caves. In 2025 Homes England bought the site to build homes.

Drury Hill

c. 1620

The street was first called Vault Lane, after the great rock cellars under the house where it met Low Pavement, and later Parkyn Lane. It took its present name about 1620 from Alderman Drury, a wealthy shoemaker who lived in that house, Vault Hall. Traffic once had to climb the cliff to the old town by Malin Hill, the Long Stairs and Hollow Stone; it found the slope of Drury Hill, up from Narrow Marsh, much easier, and the street became the town's chief business thoroughfare. When carts and carriages came into use in the 17th century it proved too narrow, and Hollow Stone was eased to become a main way into the town. At its narrowest the street was only 4 feet 10 inches wide, and a postern gate stood at its top when the town was walled. It was demolished in the 1960s, despite protests, for the Broad Marsh shopping centre.

Abel Collin

Abel Collin's almshouses

Abel Collin was an English philanthropist. He established Abel Collin's Charity.

Wikipedia: Abel Collin

Abel Collin, born in Nottingham in 1653, left money in his will of 1704 for almshouses. Those on Friar Lane, built in 1709, were demolished in 1956 to make way for Maid Marian Way; his charity, the oldest in Nottingham, still has houses in Beeston.

The Exchange

1724

In 1724–26 the town's Exchange was built in the Market Place to a design by the mayor, Marmaduke Pennell. It held the Corporation's offices, and its Great Hall was used for concerts, elections and balls. It was demolished in 1926 to make way for the Council House.

Halifax Place Wesleyan Chapel

1798

Methodists met in Nottingham from about 1740, in rooms here and there, and in 1782 moved to Hockley Chapel on Goose Gate. When the New Connexion split from the Wesleyans in 1797 and took that chapel, the Wesleyans bought land in Halifax Place and opened a chapel for 1,600 on 2 December 1798. Its successor of 1847 had the highest ceiling of any Wesleyan chapel in England; it closed in 1930 and was pulled down in 1966.

Wikipedia: Halifax Place Wesleyan Chapel

The Black Boy Hotel

A hotel on Long Row, rebuilt in 1886–88 by Watson Fothergill and enlarged around 1900. It was demolished in 1970, and a new shop building, first Littlewoods, went up in its place.

Nottingham Victoria station

built in 1899

Nottingham Victoria, the Great Central and Great Northern railways' station, opened on 24 May 1900 and closed on 4 September 1967. It was demolished except for its clock tower, which still stands at the entrance of the Victoria Centre built on the site.

Industry

Two industrial buildings that no longer stand: a tobacco warehouse and a silk mill.

Lowe's Silk Mill

During the Reform riots of 1831, Lowe's Silk Mill in Beeston was burnt on 11 October, the day after Nottingham Castle, and the same day the riots ceased.

Player's bonded warehouses

John Player built his Castle Tobacco Factories in Radford, and the firm's three bonded warehouses, seven to nine storeys high, were finished here in 1939. Tobacco making in Nottingham ended in 2016; the warehouses were demolished, the last in 2022.

Further out

Lost places beyond the centre: a priory, a church, a hall, a viaduct, a station, a spring and a manor house, and the caves of the Hermitage.

St Ann's Well

1301

The district takes its name from a spring once thought to heal. It was recorded in 1301 as the Brodewell, and was also called Robin Hood's Well; in 1500 monks built a chapel beside it and dedicated it to St Ann. A spired cover put over it in 1856 was pulled down in 1887 for the railway, and the site was later built over by the Gardeners pub.

Wikipedia: St Ann's, Nottingham

Lenton Priory

1538

William Peverel founded a priory of Cluniac monks at Lenton in about 1102–08, and richly endowed it. In February 1538 its last prior, Nicholas Heath, and many of his monks were thrown into prison, accused of high treason. The prior, eight monks and four Lenton labourers were executed, in Nottingham and in front of the priory, and the priory was dissolved that year. Little is left: the Priory Church of St Anthony is thought to keep part of its hospital chapel.

Wikipedia: Lenton Priory

The lost church of Flawford

St Peter's, the old church of Flawford, closed in 1718 and was pulled down in 1779. Three alabaster figures found in it, probably hidden around 1539, are now at Nottingham Castle Museum, and Ruddington's chapel took its name.

Bulwell Hall

1770

The landowner John Newton built Bulwell Hall in 1770. It was sold at auction in 1864 to Samuel Thomas Cooper, and was pulled down in 1956; its grounds are now Bulwell Hall Park.

New Town

1845

Until the Inclosure Act of 1845 the area was open Clay Fields. The Act let the town take 1,068 acres for housing, and by 1880 the streets of terraced houses called New Town were complete. The terraces north of Victoria Park are listed, as a once common kind of house now mostly lost to bombs and clearance.

Wikipedia: St Ann's, Nottingham

Forty Bridges

1873

Between May 1873 and November 1875 Joseph Firbank built a viaduct here for the Great Northern Railway, 43 arches and 1,716 feet long, known locally as Forty Bridges. It was pulled down in 1973 to make way for the A610 bypass.

The old Manor House

A house called the Manor House stood here, near the river at the south end of the village green, and is said to have once been a home of the Clifton family. It appears on an 1885 map. Manor House Close was built on its site; next to it stands the only house left of the row once called Manor House and Main Road Cottages.

Hall, manor and green

1906

The Charlton family, after whom the Charlton Arms is named, lived at Chilwell Hall, since pulled down. In 1906 Chilwell Manor Golf Club was laid out on land that had belonged to the manor, and the Manor House and Chilwell Green stood until 1965.

Gedling station

1960

Gedling's railway station closed on 4 April 1960, though coal trains ran to the colliery for decades more. Its building is now a youth centre, and there are plans to reopen the line or make it a cycleway.
