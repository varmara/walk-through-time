Hucknall

Hucknall was a village of framework knitters that became a mining town after its collieries opened in the 1860s. Lord Byron is buried in its parish church. From 1927 Rolls-Royce tested aero engines at its airfield, and in 2004 the tram arrived.

Hucknall Torkard

1086

Domesday Book, in 1086, calls it Hochenale. From 1295 until 1915 the town was Hucknall Torkard, after the Torcards, the leading landowners.

The Byron vault

12th century

St Mary Magdalene's stands on the site of a Saxon church. Its tower was built in stages between the 12th and 14th centuries, and its porch in 1320. Most of the Lords Byron lie in the family vault here, among them the poet Lord Byron, and his daughter Ada, Countess of Lovelace, rests there too. Byron died in Greece on 19 April 1824; Westminster Abbey refused to bury him, and after he lay in state in London he was brought here. The church is listed Grade II*.

Hucknall's pits

1861

Hucknall had been a village of framework knitters. In 1861 the Hucknall Colliery Company sank its Top Pit off Watnall Road, and in 1866 its Bottom Pit off Portland Road, and in under a century Hucknall grew into a market town. The Top Pit closed in 1943 and the Bottom Pit in 1986. A mining memorial in the town centre names both pits.

Rolls-Royce at Hucknall

1927

The airfield opened in 1916, and from 1927 Rolls-Royce used it to test its engines. In the Second World War the first P-51 Mustang fitted with a Rolls-Royce Merlin flew from here, and in December 1940 a German prisoner, Franz von Werra, tried to escape by posing as a Dutch pilot and taking a Hurricane. In the early 1950s the works built the "Flying Bedstead", a test rig for vertical take-off, whose first free flight was on 3 August 1954. The flight test centre closed in 1971.

Hucknall's battle headquarters

North of RAF Hucknall, across Watnall Road in the billet area, stood a fortified battle headquarters, from which the station would have been commanded if enemy forces attacked. All the airfield's defensive works survive.

Hucknall airfield's defences

In the Second World War the airfield was defended by a concrete pillbox with an anti-aircraft gun mounting, machine-gun posts, slit trenches and Stanton air-raid shelters, and all its defensive works survive. Which of them the bunkers here were is not known.

The trams come back, 2004

2004

Trams returned to Nottingham after 68 years when the Nottingham Express Transit network opened in 2004. Hucknall's stop opened on 9 March 2004 with the rest of the first phase, beside the station reopened on 17 May 1993 as part of the Robin Hood Line.
