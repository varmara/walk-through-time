Thomas Hawksley

1866

The water engineer Thomas Hawksley was born at Arnot Hill House in 1807. His Trent Bridge waterworks, completed in 1831, gave Britain its first high-pressure constant supply, which kept dirty water out of the mains.

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton

Trent Bridge

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton MICE, FGS, FRMS, was born in Leeds on 6 December 1834 and died in Nottingham on 6 March 1887. He was Borough Engineer for Nottingham from 1859.

Wikipedia: Marriott Ogle Tarbotton

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton was Nottingham's Borough Engineer from 1859. He designed Trent Bridge, planned the town's underground sewers, culverted the River Leen and built the viaduct over the railway on Carrington Street in 1866.

Papplewick Pumping Station

1881

In 1881–84 a pumping station was built here to raise drinking water for Nottingham from deep wells. It had two beam engines by James Watt & Co. and an engine house by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton, ornate with cast iron and stained glass. It pumped until 1969 and opened as a museum in 1976.

Science

A miller who became a mathematician, a library, a college built in 1877–81, and the university where magnetic resonance imaging was developed.

Bromley House

1752

A Georgian town house was built here in 1752. The Nottingham Subscription Library, founded in 1816, moved in during 1821. In 1836 a meridian line was laid so that the library's longcase clock could keep Nottingham time, 4 minutes and 33 seconds behind Greenwich, and it still does. In 1841 Alfred Barber opened the first photographic studio in the Midlands in its attics.

George Green

Bromley House, Green's Windmill

George Green was a British mathematical physicist born in Sneinton, Nottinghamshire, now part of the city of Nottingham. Despite receiving little recognition during his lifetime, he is best remembered for his Essay on electricity and magnetism published in 1828.

Wikipedia: George Green (mathematician)

George Green, the mathematician, was a miller: his family's windmill in Sneinton is restored and named after him. He was a member of the subscription library at Bromley House.

Edward Joseph Lowe

Plaque to Edward Joseph Lowe

Edward Joseph Lowe FRS FGS FRAS FLS was a British botanist, meteorologist and astronomer, who published papers on a wide variety of subjects, including luminous meteors, sunspots, the zodiacal light, meteorological observations during the 1860 solar eclipse, conchology, ferns, grasses and other plants.

Mary Kendrick

Curzon Street

Mary Patricia Kendrick MBE born Mary Patricia Boak was a British tidal engineer who was an expert on silt. She worked on many projects but she is known for leading a team working on the Thames Barrier. She broke a 200 year long list of Admirals who looked after keeping the River Mersey navigable when she appointed Acting Conservator of the River Mersey - a role that dates back to 1625.

Wikipedia: Mary Kendrick

Mary Kendrick, the tidal engineer who led the studies of river levels and silt for the Thames Barrier, grew up on Curzon Street, where her father was a haulage contractor. She later kept the Mersey navigable as its Acting Conservator.

Wikipedia: Mary Kendrick

The MRI scanner

Peter Mansfield came to the University of Nottingham in 1964 and developed magnetic resonance imaging here. Just before Christmas 1978 a full-body scanner was installed, and he made the first scan of a live patient. He shared the Nobel Prize in 2003 with Paul Lauterbur.

Medicine

Doctors, nurses and hospitals: the General Hospital of 1782, Nottingham's first woman GP, the making of ibuprofen and the asylum at Mapperley.

Nottingham General Hospital

1782

A legacy from the banker John Key paid for a hospital on part of Nottingham Park. Its first building, designed by John Simpson, was begun on 12 February 1781 and opened with 44 beds in September 1782; John Wesley was an early visitor. In 1844 it took in the hurt when a stand collapsed at a hanging on Garner's Hill, killing twelve. Hine added a storey in 1855, and Alfred Waterhouse's round Jubilee Wing opened in 1900. It closed in 1992, its work moved to the Queen's Medical Centre.

Wikipedia: Nottingham General Hospital

Mary Potter

Hyson Green

The Little Company of Mary, also known as the Blue Sisters, is a Catholic religious institute of women dedicated to caring for the suffering, the sick, and the dying. The order was founded in 1877 in Nottingham, England by Mary Potter.

Mapperley Hospital

1880

George Thomas Hine designed the Nottingham Borough Lunatic Asylum, opened in August 1880 with its wards along one long corridor. It was extended in 1889, joined the NHS as Mapperley Hospital in 1948, and closed in December 1994. The north end is now Duncan Macmillan House, the local NHS trust's headquarters, and the south end, Nightingale House, is flats.

Wikipedia: Mapperley Hospital

15 Regent Street

Margaret Glen-Bott (1891–1969) came to Nottingham in 1916. The first woman gynaecologist to be Honorary Surgeon at the Women's Hospital and the Children's Hospital, and later a magistrate, she lived at 15 Regent Street.

Louisa May Long

Florence Boot Hall

Florence Boot, born in St Helier, Jersey, in 1863, married Jesse Boot in 1886 and helped him run the firm. She brought books and stationery into the Goose Gate shop and started Boots Book-Lovers' Library and cafés in the stores. She founded the university's first hall for women, named Florence Boot Hall after her.

Wikipedia: Florence Boot

Louisa May Long (1892–1981), who nursed with the 94 Nottingham VAD at Radcliffe on Trent in the First World War, was Matron of Florence Boot Hall from 1928 to 1946.

Sarah Gray

21 Regent Street

Sarah Gray, Nottingham's first woman GP, lived and practised at 21 Regent Street. At the Women's Hospital from 1899 she was refused charge of in-patients; in 1921 she became the first president of the Nottingham Medico-Chirurgical Society.

Ibuprofen

Ibuprofen was first made at Boots in Nottingham in December 1961, by Stewart Adams's team. Their later laboratory, on Pennyfoot Street, is now BioCity, and in 2013 a Royal Society of Chemistry blue plaque marked the discovery.
