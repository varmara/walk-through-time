Almshouses and hospitals, 1241–1392

By 1241 Nottingham's Hospital of St John the Baptist had a rule of life for its brothers and sisters, laid down by Walter de Grey, Archbishop of York; it stood at the corner of today's Parliament Street and Glasshouse Street.

Plumptre Hospital

1392

In 1392 John de Plumptre, a Mayor of Nottingham, founded a hospital here for "thirteen poor women broken down of age and depressed of poverty", with two priests. Huntingdon Plumptre repaired it in 1650, and the present building was begun in 1823. The charity closed in 1991, when it could no longer afford to improve the building, and in 2001 the Royal National Institute for the Blind took it over.

Learning and healing, 1752–1821

Hospitals of this time were voluntary: they were paid for by subscriptions, gifts and fundraising. By the middle of the 18th century there were six in London.

Bromley House

1752

A Georgian town house was built here in 1752. The Nottingham Subscription Library, founded in 1816, moved in during 1821. In 1836 a meridian line was laid so that the library's longcase clock could keep Nottingham time, 4 minutes and 33 seconds behind Greenwich, and it still does. In 1841 Alfred Barber opened the first photographic studio in the Midlands in its attics.

Nottingham General Hospital

1782

A legacy from the banker John Key paid for a hospital on part of Nottingham Park. Its first building, designed by John Simpson, was begun on 12 February 1781 and opened with 44 beds in September 1782; John Wesley was an early visitor. In 1844 it took in the hurt from a hanging on High Pavement, where twelve died on Garner's Hill: Wikipedia says a stand collapsed, but the newspapers of the day describe the crowd being forced down the steps into Garner's Hill and trampled. Hine added a storey in 1855, and Alfred Waterhouse's round Jubilee Wing opened in 1900. It closed in 1992, its work moved to the Queen's Medical Centre.

Self-taught science, 1793–1880s

From 1821 mechanics' institutes taught working men science and technical subjects, with libraries and apparatus for experiments; by the middle of the century there were over 700 in Britain and abroad.

George Green

Bromley House, Green's Windmill

George Green was a British mathematical physicist born in Sneinton, Nottinghamshire, now part of the city of Nottingham. Despite receiving little recognition during his lifetime, he is best remembered for his Essay on electricity and magnetism published in 1828.

Wikipedia: George Green (mathematician)

George Green, the mathematician, was a miller: his family's windmill in Sneinton is restored and named after him. He was a member of the subscription library at Bromley House.

Clean water, 1831–1884

Cholera reached Britain in October 1831. The epidemic led in time to the Public Health Act of 1848.

Thomas Hawksley

1866

The water engineer Thomas Hawksley was born at Arnot Hill House in 1807. His Trent Bridge waterworks, completed in 1831, gave Britain its first high-pressure constant supply, which kept dirty water out of the mains.

Papplewick Pumping Station

1881

In 1881–84 a pumping station was built here to raise drinking water for Nottingham from deep wells. It had two beam engines by James Watt & Co. and an engine house by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton, ornate with cast iron and stained glass. It pumped until 1969 and opened as a museum in 1976.

Mapperley Hospital

1880

George Thomas Hine designed the Nottingham Borough Lunatic Asylum, opened in August 1880 with its wards along one long corridor. It was extended in 1889, joined the NHS as Mapperley Hospital in 1948, and closed in December 1994. The north end is now Duncan Macmillan House, the local NHS trust's headquarters, and the south end, Nightingale House, is flats.

Women in medicine, 1899–1930s

From 11 August 1876 the Medical Act let every British medical authority license qualified applicants, women as well as men.

Sarah Gray

21 Regent Street

Sarah Gray, Nottingham's first woman GP, lived and practised at 21 Regent Street. At the Women's Hospital from 1899 she was refused charge of in-patients; in 1921 she became the first president of the Nottingham Medico-Chirurgical Society.

Discoveries, 1944–2018

Alexander Fleming found penicillin in 1928; Howard Florey and Ernst Chain's team at Oxford made it into a medicine, and the three shared the Nobel Prize in 1945. During the war it became an important part of the Allied effort, saving thousands of lives.

Penicillin at Daleside Road, 1944

1944

In the Second World War the Ministry of Supply asked Boots to design and run a plant that would make penicillin in quantity, by growing the mould on the surface of its broth. Boots built it on Daleside Road, and made huge amounts of penicillin there during the war. It was the largest surface-culture plant in the country, or by another account in the world, and one of the first penicillin factories built anywhere. Its monthly production reports run from August 1944. In 1945 a newly qualified pharmacist, Stewart Adams, later the leader of the team that found ibuprofen, took his first job there.

Boots' research houses, West Bridgford

1920

In 1920 Boots bought a site on Oakfields Road for pharmacological and bacteriological research, which was done here until new laboratories were built in 1956; the site was sold in 1964. With its city-centre labs damaged in the war, Boots put research into converted houses in West Bridgford. From 1953 to 1959 Stewart Adams worked in one of the front rooms of a house here, a rather primitive laboratory, while his technician used the larder as an office. On Rutland Road, in August 1958, his team began to study the phenoxy acids, the line of work that led to ibuprofen. The day nursery on the corner of Oakfields Road and Rutland Road was also part of Boots.

Mary Kendrick

Curzon Street

Mary Patricia Kendrick MBE born Mary Patricia Boak was a British tidal engineer who was an expert on silt. She worked on many projects but she is known for leading a team working on the Thames Barrier. She broke a 200 year long list of Admirals who looked after keeping the River Mersey navigable when she appointed Acting Conservator of the River Mersey - a role that dates back to 1625.

Wikipedia: Mary Kendrick

Mary Kendrick, the tidal engineer who led the studies of river levels and silt for the Thames Barrier, grew up on Curzon Street, where her father was a haulage contractor. She later kept the Mersey navigable as its Acting Conservator.

Ibuprofen

Ibuprofen was first made at Boots in Nottingham in December 1961, by Stewart Adams's team. Their later laboratory, on Pennyfoot Street, is now BioCity, and in 2013 a Royal Society of Chemistry blue plaque marked the discovery. Adams and the chemist John Nicholson filed the patent in 1961; the drug was first sold in Britain in 1969, as Brufen, and in 1983 it became the first such painkiller apart from aspirin sold over the counter in the UK.

The MRI scanner

Peter Mansfield came to the University of Nottingham in 1964 and developed magnetic resonance imaging here. Just before Christmas 1978 a full-body scanner was installed, and he made the first scan of a live patient. He shared the Nobel Prize in 2003 with Paul Lauterbur.

Queen's Medical Centre

1977

Queen Elizabeth II opened the Queen's Medical Centre on 28 July 1977, and it took its first patient the next year. It was the first hospital in the UK built to be a teaching hospital, with the University of Nottingham's medical and nursing schools on the same site, and with more than 1,300 beds it was long the largest hospital in the country.

A brain scanner you can wear, 2018

2018

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) maps the brain at work from the tiny magnetic fields its cells make, but its scanners weighed about half a tonne, because their sensors had to be kept at −269 °C, and the patient had to keep still. In March 2018 researchers at the Sir Peter Mansfield Imaging Centre, with UCL, showed in Nature a scanner worn like a helmet, built from new "quantum" sensors in a 3D-printed shell. Wearing it, people could nod, stretch, drink tea and even play ping pong while their brains were scanned.
