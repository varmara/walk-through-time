Canal and carriage, 1796–1919

The great age of canal building in England ran from the 1790s to the 1810s, much of it paid for by mine owners and merchants who had goods to ship.

The Nottingham Canal

built in 1895

Authorised in 1792 and engineered by William Jessop, the canal opened in 1796 from the Trent to Langley Mill, bringing coal to the town. Railways took its trade; most of the main line was abandoned in 1937.

The Park Tunnel

1855

The tunnel was meant to let carriages from the Park reach Derby Road through the sandstone. It was begun in about 1844 and finished by T. C. Hine in 1855–56, but at 1 in 12 it was too steep for horses and carriages, so it became a footway. It runs from Tunnel Road to Derby Road, with steps halfway up to Upper College Street.

Fellows Morton & Clayton

1895

Fellows Morton & Clayton, who ran the largest fleet of barges and narrowboats in the country, built this canalside depot in 1895. Boats loaded and unloaded at the wharf beside it; the building is now a pub, and listed Grade II.

The railway comes, 1832–1880

In the railway mania of the 1840s, 263 Acts of Parliament were passed to set up new railway companies in 1846 alone, and about a third of the lines they allowed were never built.

The railway that began in a pub

In 1832 coal owners meeting at the Sun Inn, Eastwood, proposed extending the Mansfield and Pinxton Railway to Leicester. It became the Midland Counties Railway, whose line from Nottingham, at Carrington Street, to Derby opened in 1839.

Long Eaton and the railway

1839

Long Eaton stayed a village until the Midland Counties Railway came in 1839. A great railway yard, Toton Sidings, grew up just north of the town, and by 1900 it had more than 10,000 people.

Forty Bridges

1873

Between May 1873 and November 1875 Joseph Firbank built a viaduct here for the Great Northern Railway, 43 arches and 1,716 feet long, known locally as Forty Bridges. It was pulled down in 1973 to make way for the A610 bypass.

Bennerley Viaduct

1877

In 1876–77 a viaduct was built here of wrought iron, light enough for ground undermined by coal workings. It closed in 1968 but was too costly to take down, and in 2022 it reopened to walkers and cyclists. The only other viaduct like it is Meldon Viaduct in Devon.

Lady Bay Bridge

1878

Built in 1878 to carry the Midland Railway's direct line from Nottingham to Melton Mowbray across the Trent. When the line was abandoned in 1968 the crossing was turned into a road bridge, finished in 1979.

Edwalton station

The station opened in 1880 on the Midland Railway's direct line from Nottingham to Melton Mowbray, which let its London expresses avoid Leicester. It closed to passengers in 1941, and its site is now housing. Beyond it the line became the Old Dalby test track, where the Advanced Passenger Train was tried.

The last main line, 1899–1904

The Great Central's line to London, opened in 1899, was the last main line built in Britain until High Speed 1 in 2003. Most of it closed between 1966 and 1969.

Gotham's gypsum

1899

Gypsum was dug from Gotham Hill and made into plaster. A branch line about two miles long, opened in March 1899, ran west from the Great Central main line to the works; it closed in the early 1960s and is now a footpath. The pin is approximate.

built in 1899

Nottingham Victoria, the Great Central and Great Northern railways' station, opened on 24 May 1900 and closed on 4 September 1967. It was demolished except for its clock tower, which still stands at the entrance of the Victoria Centre built on the site.

Nottingham station

1904

Nottingham's first station, opened in 1839 by the Midland Counties Railway, stood across Carrington Street; a through station replaced it on this site in 1848. The present building, of red brick and terracotta, was designed by the local architect Albert Edward Lambert and opened on 16 January 1904. Its carriage entrances have Art Nouveau gates of wrought iron. A pair taken down in 1951, made in Leicester in 1904, is now in the Science Museum Group's collection.

Trams, buses and trolleybuses, 1900–1966

A company had run horse and steam trams in Nottingham from 1877; the Corporation's electric trams began on 1 January 1901.

Sherwood tram depot

1900

Nottingham Corporation Tramways built the depot in 1900 when it carried the Carrington tram line on up Mansfield Road to Winchester Street. Trams ran until 6 September 1936, when buses replaced them. Part of the building is now the Samuel Hall pub, named after the man who laid out Sherwood.

Wikipedia: Sherwood, Nottingham

The trolleybuses

1927

Trolleybuses, buses drawing power from overhead wires, first ran on 10 April 1927 and took over the tram routes one by one; the last tram ran in September 1936. A depot opened on Parliament Street in June 1929 and is still in use. By the end of 1937 the city had 125 trolleybuses, the largest fleet in the country, and at most 157. The last ran on 30 June 1966.

Into the air, 1916–1930

King George V founded the Royal Flying Corps on 13 April 1912. By the end of March 1918 it had some 150 squadrons, and on 1 April it was joined with the navy's air service as the Royal Air Force.

Rolls-Royce at Hucknall

1927

The airfield opened in 1916, and from 1927 Rolls-Royce used it to test its engines. In the Second World War the first P-51 Mustang fitted with a Rolls-Royce Merlin flew from here, and in December 1940 a German prisoner, Franz von Werra, tried to escape by posing as a Dutch pilot and taking a Hurricane. In the early 1950s the works built the "Flying Bedstead", a test rig for vertical take-off, whose first free flight was on 3 August 1954. The flight test centre closed in 1971.

Wikipedia: Hucknall

Tollerton airfield

1941

Licensed in 1929 and opened in 1930 by Sir Sefton Brancker, it was one of Britain's first municipal aerodromes. In the Second World War it repaired Lancaster bombers; its 18 concrete pillboxes are all listed. The airfield closed on 6 June 2025, sold to the Vistry Group to build 4,000 houses.

Closures, 1947–1972

In 1963 the Beeching report picked out 2,363 stations and 5,000 miles of line for closure, and most of them closed as planned.

Gedling station

1960

Gedling's railway station closed on 4 April 1960, though coal trains ran to the colliery for decades more. Its building is now a youth centre, and there are plans to reopen the line or make it a cycleway.

Coming back, 1980s–2017

Britain's first modern street-running tramway opened in Manchester in 1992; until then Blackpool's, of 1885, was the only one of the first trams still running. A few of the railways and stations closed in the 1960s have reopened too.

The marina on the sidings

1980s

West of the town the canal ran past railway sidings. In the 1980s the sidings were dug out for Castle Marina, a basin of moorings off the canal, with shops and offices round it.

No station for fifty years

2017

Ilkeston once had three stations. The Town station closed to passengers in 1947, the North in 1964 and the Junction in January 1967, leaving the town without a station for fifty years. After a long campaign a new Ilkeston station was approved in 2013 and, delayed by flood works and great crested newts, opened on 2 April 2017.
