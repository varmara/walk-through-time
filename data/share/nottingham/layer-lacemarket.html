The Danes in Nottingham, 868

868

In 868 a Viking army under Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan Ragnarsson took Nottingham and wintered here. Burgred of Mercia and his West Saxon allies laid siege, but made peace and let them go in 869. From 877 the Danes settled, and Nottingham, a town of about 39 acres, became one of the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw, until Edward of Wessex took it in 918. King Edmund won the Five Boroughs back for the English in 942.

Weekday Cross

1529

Before the Old Market Square, Weekday Cross, by the Guild Hall, was the town's main market and its centre. A cross went up here in about 1529–30, and by 1711 people called it "the Pillar". Butter, eggs, pigeons, wild fowl, fruit and fish were sold on Wednesdays and Fridays. The Corporation had the cross pulled down and sold in 1804; Nottingham Civic Society put up a new one in 1993. The Guild Hall's site is now Nottingham Contemporary's.

Wikipedia: Weekday Cross

Shire Hall

1375

Courts sat on this spot from at least 1375, and prisoners were held here from at least 1449. The hall was rebuilt to a design by James Gandon in 1769–72, and largely rebuilt again after a fire in 1876. In 1864 Richard Thomas Parker was hanged on a scaffold over the front steps, the last public execution here. Courts sat here until 1991, and in 1995 the building opened as a museum, now the National Justice Museum.

City of Caves

1500

In 1500 a tannery was set up in two caves opening onto the cliff face, the only known underground tannery in Britain. Over the years the caves under the old town were also used as pub cellars and as an air raid shelter.

Broad Marsh

1539

This was boggy ground on the edge of the medieval town, where the Franciscan friary of Greyfriars stood until it was closed in 1539. After large-scale slum clearance, the Broad Marsh shopping centre was officially opened on 25 March 1975. It closed in 2020 and was left partially demolished; caves found beneath it became part of the City of Caves. In 2025 Homes England bought the site to build homes.

Drury Hill

c. 1620

The street was first called Vault Lane, after the great rock cellars under the house where it met Low Pavement, and later Parkyn Lane. It took its present name about 1620 from Alderman Drury, a wealthy shoemaker who lived in that house, Vault Hall. Traffic once had to climb the cliff to the old town by Malin Hill, the Long Stairs and Hollow Stone; it found the slope of Drury Hill, up from Narrow Marsh, much easier, and the street became the town's chief business thoroughfare. When carts and carriages came into use in the 17th century it proved too narrow, and Hollow Stone was eased to become a main way into the town. At its narrowest the street was only 4 feet 10 inches wide, and a postern gate stood at its top when the town was walled. It was demolished in the 1960s, despite protests, for the Broad Marsh shopping centre.

Halifax Place Wesleyan Chapel

1798

Methodists met in Nottingham from about 1740, in rooms here and there, and in 1782 moved to Hockley Chapel on Goose Gate. When the New Connexion split from the Wesleyans in 1797 and took that chapel, the Wesleyans bought land in Halifax Place and opened a chapel for 1,600 on 2 December 1798. Its successor of 1847 had the highest ceiling of any Wesleyan chapel in England; it closed in 1930 and was pulled down in 1966.

Adams Building

1855

Built from 1854 to designs by Thomas Chambers Hine for Thomas Adams, a Quaker lace manufacturer, and opened in 1855 as his lace showroom and warehouse, with a chapel for the workers in the basement. Inspectors thought it a model example of its kind; since 1999 it has been part of Nottingham College.

Watson Fothergill's offices

1895

Watson Fothergill, born Fothergill Watson, took his mother's family name in 1892 and built his own offices here, on George Street, in 1895. Its front has a statue of a medieval architect and busts of Augustus Pugin and George Edmund Street; it is listed Grade II.

Nottingham station

1904

Nottingham's first station, opened in 1839 by the Midland Counties Railway, stood across Carrington Street; a through station replaced it on this site in 1848. The present building, of red brick and terracotta, was designed by the local architect Albert Edward Lambert and opened on 16 January 1904.

The Nottingham Blitz, 1941

built in 1377

On the night of 8–9 May 1941 the Luftwaffe bombed Nottingham. Firefighters saved St Mary's Church after an incendiary burned through its roof; three Boots factories, in Poplar Street, Island Street and Station Street, were on fire; University College and the Shakespeare Street chapel were hit. The worst loss was at the Co-op bakery on Meadow Lane, where 49 people died. In all, 159 people were killed that night.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Blitz

Nottingham Contemporary

2009

Garners Hill, said to be the oldest site in Nottingham, once held cave dwellings, a Saxon fort and a medieval town hall, until the Victorians cleared it for a railway line. On 14 November 2009 an art centre designed by Caruso St John opened here, its walls embossed with a Nottingham lace pattern.
