Risings and riots

For a century and more, Nottingham's people took to the streets again and again. The town lived by hosiery, settled there after 1730, and later by lace, trades in which work was notoriously precarious. Few of those workers had a vote: before 1832 only about one Briton in twenty did. And the town grew so fast and so badly that it came to have the worst slums in England. Each chapter here is one outbreak, set against what lay behind it.

The cheese riot, 1766

Food riots were a familiar part of 18th-century life, with at least twelve major outbreaks. In 1766 poor harvests had already pushed up the price of grain, and when the first large-scale exports of it began that August, riots broke out across England; eight people were shot dead putting them down. Nottingham's came at its October fair.

Goose Fair

A royal charter of 1284 refers to Nottingham's fairs. For centuries Goose Fair was held in the Old Market Square, until in 1928, having outgrown the city centre, it moved to the Forest Recreation Ground. It was cancelled for the Great Plague in 1646 and during the World Wars.

The cheese riot, 1766

1766

After a poor harvest in 1766, food was dear and people feared shortages. At Goose Fair that October, when Lincolnshire merchants began carting away cheese they had bought, the townspeople tried to stop them; the fair turned to a riot, shops, a warehouse and a boat were looted, and wheels of cheese were rolled down the streets. The mayor could not restore order, and soldiers fired on the crowd, killing William Eggleston, a farmer guarding his own goods. It took several days to bring the town back to order.

The Luddites, 1811

Stocking-knitters had taken organised action at times since 1675. The years 1810 to 1812 were hard ones, with high unemployment and inflation, made worse by the cost of the wars with Napoleon. The frame-breakers took their name from Ned Ludd, a probably mythical apprentice said to have smashed two stocking frames in 1779, and wrote as Captain, General or King Ludd.

The first Luddites

The Luddite movement began in Arnold on 11 March 1811. Across Nottinghamshire the frame-breakers went after the "wide" knitting frames used to make cheap goods. That first evening, framework knitters gathered in Nottingham's Market Place to hear speeches, marched up Mansfield Road to Arnold, and broke 63 stocking frames at the shops of the hosiers they most hated.

A Luddite shot at Bulwell, 1811

1811

On the night of 10 November 1811 some 20 to 30 frame-breakers walked from Arnold to Bulwell Forest, met about 40 more, and marched on the house of the hosier Edward Hollingworth, who had barricaded it and armed his friends. Shots were exchanged in the dark; the Luddites drove the defenders out through a back window and smashed all seven frames, with the doors, windows and furniture. At dawn the body of one of the attackers lay by the road at Bulwell Forest: John Westley, a framework knitter who had lived in Arnold for twelve years. The inquest found the killing justifiable, in self-defence. As far as is known, he was the only man killed in the Nottinghamshire disturbances.

The Pentrich rising, 1817

Peace with France in 1815 brought no relief. Industrial change, demobilised soldiers and Corn Laws that kept bread dear drove the country into a deep depression. Radicals at Pentrich had their own Hampden Club, and the Home Office watched them through local magistrates and paid informers. The Pentrich men hoped to join a march on London in support of Sir Francis Burdett's bill for parliamentary reform.

The Nottingham Captain

Jeremiah Brandreth, born in Wilford, led the Pentrich rising of 1817 and was known as the Nottingham Captain. He was hanged for high treason outside Derby Gaol that November, and then beheaded.

The Pentrich rising, 1817

On the night of 9–10 June 1817, men from around Pentrich in Derbyshire set out from South Wingfield to march on Nottingham, led by Jeremiah Brandreth, the Nottingham Captain. At Giltbrook they met a small force of Light Dragoons and scattered. The news that had set them marching was a hoax by William Oliver, a government informer; Brandreth and two others were hanged and beheaded at Derby.

The Reform riots, 1831

Parliament's seats had not been redrawn for population in many decades: some boroughs had a handful of voters, while Manchester had no MP at all. A bill to reform it passed the Commons in 1831, and its defeat in the Lords set off full-scale riots in Bristol and Derby as well as Nottingham.

The castle burned, 1831

1674–79

When the House of Lords threw out the Second Reform Bill on 8 October 1831, rioting broke out in Nottingham and a crowd burned the castle, which belonged to the Duke of Newcastle, an opponent of reform. Three men were hanged; the Duke refused to restore the castle, and its burnt shell stood on the rock for years.

Colwick Hall, 1831

built in 1831

On 10 October 1831, during the Reform riots, a crowd marched on Colwick Hall, the home of John Musters, and sacked and partly burned it. His wife, Mary Chaworth Musters, spent the night in pouring rain with her daughter Sophia, hiding beneath the shrubbery; she died at Wiverton Hall some four months later, from the shock. The hall is now a hotel.

The Chartists, 1842–1847

The People's Charter of 1838 asked for six reforms, among them a vote for every man over 21, the secret ballot and pay for MPs. The Commons refused to hear its first petition, of 1.3 million signatures, in 1839, and rejected the second, of over three million, in May 1842. That summer, after employers had cut wages by up to a quarter in the slump, a general strike spread from Stalybridge and Ashton to Manchester and beyond.

Sturge and Walter, 1842

1842

When George Larpent resigned his Nottingham seat, Joseph Sturge stood at the by-election: a reformer whose Complete Suffrage Union, a more middle-class rival to the Chartists' own association, campaigned for votes for all men. He lost, and the Conservative John Walter was elected on 4 August 1842; Walter was unseated on petition the next year, and the Liberal Thomas Gisborne won the seat in April 1843. Five years later Nottingham elected the Chartist leader Feargus O'Connor. Where the poll was held is not known.

The Battle of Mapperley Hills, 1842

1842

In August 1842 a meeting of more than two thousand in the Market Place voted to stop work until the People's Charter became law. The magistrates banned further meetings, and for days troops and police broke up gatherings across the town. On 23 August Chartists from Hucknall, Bulwell, Arnold, Basford and other places met on the common on Mapperley Hills; the 2nd Dragoon Guards were sent, and when the crowd, sitting on the grass to eat, would not disperse, Colonel Rolleston had about four hundred arrested. As they were marched into the town, a crowd stoned the soldiers and police. About fifty went for trial, some getting hard labour. Afterwards the town's workers turned instead against the Corn Laws and the price of bread.

Feargus O'Connor

1847 · Statue of Feargus O'Connor

Feargus Edward O'Connor was an Irish Chartist leader, MP and advocate of the Land Plan, which sought to provide smallholdings for the labouring classes. A highly charismatic figure, O'Connor was admired for his energy and oratory, but was criticised for alleged egotism. His newspaper Northern Star was widely read among workers, becoming the voice of the Chartist movement.

Wikipedia: Feargus O'Connor

Feargus O'Connor, the leader of Chartism and owner of its newspaper, the Northern Star, was elected MP for Nottingham in 1847, defeating Thomas Benjamin Hobhouse. His statue by J. B. Robinson was put up in the Arboretum in 1859.

The Bogs, 1872

Enclosure turned land held in common into private property. Nottingham's burgesses, who held common rights in the town's fields, had held off their enclosure from 1787 until 1845, though it kept the town from growing outwards. Who made the attempt on Bulwell's Bogs is unclear: Samuel Thomas Cooper, lord of the manor since he bought Bulwell Hall, had died the year before, in 1871.

The Bogs

1872

The Bogs, the wet meadow by the Leen, were common land. When the lord of the manor tried to enclose them in 1872, about a thousand people marched on the land to claim it, peacefully: after a short march they sat down to lunch by the river. The Corporation later bought the land for the town. A bigger play-park was built in 2003, and the park won a Green Flag Award in 2004.

The St Ann's riots, 1958

St Ann's had been built after 1845 on the town's old Clay Fields, for the working poor, in terraces around courts; most of its houses had no inside toilet. After the war people came from the Caribbean to work, in growing numbers from 1955, and met racism; by 1958 about one in ten of St Ann's people was not white.

The St Ann's riots, 1958

On 23 August 1958, at closing time, a fight outside a pub in St Ann's grew into a riot between white and West Indian residents; the police restored order within 90 minutes. Some 4,500 people took part in the disturbances to some extent, and a crowd of about 4,000 gathered the following weekend. The Chief Constable, Athelstan Popkess, played the events down afterwards and said they were not about race. With the Notting Hill riots a week later, the disturbances led to talks between Caribbean governments and the British government.
