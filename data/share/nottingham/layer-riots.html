The cheese riot, 1766

1766

After a poor harvest in 1766, food was dear and people feared shortages. At Goose Fair that October, when Lincolnshire merchants began carting away cheese they had bought, the townspeople tried to stop them; the fair turned to a riot, shops, a warehouse and a boat were looted, and wheels of cheese were rolled down the streets. The mayor could not restore order, and soldiers fired on the crowd, killing William Eggleston, a farmer guarding his own goods. It took several days to bring the town back to order.

The first Luddites

The Luddite movement began in Arnold on 11 March 1811. Across Nottinghamshire the frame-breakers went after the "wide" knitting frames used to make cheap goods.

The Nottingham Captain

Jeremiah Brandreth, born in Wilford, led the Pentrich rising of 1817 and was known as the Nottingham Captain. He was hanged for high treason outside Derby Gaol that November, and then beheaded.

The Pentrich rising, 1817

On the night of 9–10 June 1817, men from around Pentrich in Derbyshire set out from South Wingfield to march on Nottingham, led by Jeremiah Brandreth, the Nottingham Captain. At Giltbrook they met a small force of Light Dragoons and scattered. The news that had set them marching was a hoax by William Oliver, a government informer; Brandreth and two others were hanged and beheaded at Derby.

The castle burned, 1831

1674–79

When the House of Lords threw out the Second Reform Bill on 8 October 1831, rioting broke out in Nottingham and a crowd burned the castle, which belonged to the Duke of Newcastle, an opponent of reform. Three men were hanged; the Duke refused to restore the castle, and its burnt shell stood on the rock for years.

The Battle of Mapperley Hills, 1842

1842

In August 1842 a meeting of more than two thousand in the Market Place voted to stop work until the People's Charter became law. The magistrates banned further meetings, and for days troops and police broke up gatherings across the town. On 23 August Chartists from Hucknall, Bulwell, Arnold, Basford and other places met on the common on Mapperley Hills; the 2nd Dragoon Guards were sent, and when the crowd, sitting on the grass to eat, would not disperse, Colonel Rolleston had about four hundred arrested. As they were marched into the town, a crowd stoned the soldiers and police. About fifty went for trial, some getting hard labour. Afterwards the town's workers turned instead against the Corn Laws and the price of bread. The pin is approximate.

The Bogs

1872

The Bogs, the wet meadow by the Leen, were common land. When the lord of the manor tried to enclose them in 1872, about a thousand people marched on the land to claim it, and the Corporation later bought it for the town. The park won a Green Flag Award in 2004.

The St Ann's riots, 1958

On 23 August 1958 a fight in St Ann's grew into a riot between white and West Indian residents; the police restored order within 90 minutes. A crowd of about 4,000 gathered the following weekend. With the Notting Hill riots a week later, the disturbances led to talks between Caribbean governments and the British government. The pin is approximate.
