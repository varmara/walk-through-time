Roman times

Before the Romans this was the land of the Corieltauvi, a Celtic tribe of the East Midlands whose capital was at Leicester, and there is little sign that they fought the conquest. No Roman town stood where Nottingham is now: the traces of those centuries lie around it.

The army, 1st century AD

For the first few decades after the invasion of AD 43, the Fosse Way, which crossed this county, marked the western frontier of Roman rule.

Roman Broxtowe

1st century

Workmen laying the sewers and roads of the new Broxtowe council estate found Roman coins and pottery. That was in 1937. Later finds included a mount from the scabbard of a Roman sword, and in 1964 a ditch gave up pottery and pieces of a lava quern of the 1st century AD. The site has been taken for an early Roman fort of about AD 50 to 75. Another view is that it was a British hut settlement, not a fort.

Roman Calverton

Roman times

Aerial photographs show traces of two Roman marching camps in a field north-east of the Oxton Road and Whinbush Lane crossroads: a smaller one of four acres inside a larger one of about twenty-six acres. Nothing shows above ground, and the site is a scheduled monument. Camps like these were dug by an army unit for an overnight stop. Two very similar hoards of Roman coins were later found in the village, less than three hundred yards apart: in June 1959, while foundations were dug for Manor Park Infants' School on Collyer Road, and in about April 1960, while a house was built in Crookdole Lane. The first was buried in an earthenware pot.

Wikipedia: Calverton, Nottinghamshire

Farms and villas, 1st–4th centuries

The Romans brought better farming, and by the 4th century lowland Britain was prosperous enough to send grain across the sea. That wealth paid for a blossoming of villa building and decoration between AD 300 and 350.

A Roman farm by the A453

1st century

Digging before the A453 was widened, in 2012 and 2013, uncovered a farm on rising ground south of the old road between Barton in Fabis and Clifton. A large enclosure was made here in the 1st century AD, around the time of the Roman conquest. In the 3rd century a smaller enclosure replaced it, with a building for drying and processing grain, and the farm declined in the 4th century. Each period left one burial.

Glebe Farm Roman villa

1st century

A Roman villa of the winged-corridor kind stood at Glebe Farm, lived in from the 1st to the 4th century. In 1858 a mosaic floor about 15 feet by 10 was found under the soil here, and charred wood suggested the house had burned. The Castle Museum turned the floor down as a gift in 1878, as moving it would have cost a few pounds. It was later lifted and taken to the rectory, and has since been lost.

Roman Chilwell

Roman times

Roman buildings, pottery and coins have been found in Chilwell. It began as a hamlet on the road from Nottingham to Ashby-de-la-Zouch, and is in the Domesday Book of 1086.

By the rivers, Roman times

Roman builders paved fords with flagstones on timber piles, as at Littleborough further down the Trent, where a Roman road crossed the river to the town of Segelocum.

The temple on Red Hill

Roman times

A Roman temple stood on Red Hill, above the meeting of the Soar and the Trent, perhaps with a small town beside it, and at least three curse tablets have been found at the site. Remains of a Roman villa and other buildings, and Roman pottery, have also been found in Ratcliffe.
