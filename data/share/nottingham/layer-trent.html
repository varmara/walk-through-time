Crossing the Trent

The Trent is the third-longest river in the United Kingdom, and it once marked the boundary between the north of England and the south. Nottingham grew where it could be crossed.

Fords, c. 1000 BC–AD 400

Long before any bridge, people crossed where the river ran shallow. The Trent is known for sudden floods after storms and spring snowmelt, and its flood records go back some 900 years.

A bridge against the Danes, 868–924

Edward the Elder of Wessex ended Danish rule in Nottingham with an assault in the summer of 918.

The Danes in Nottingham, 868

868

In 868 a Viking army under Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan Ragnarsson took Nottingham and wintered here. Burgred of Mercia and his West Saxon allies laid siege, but made peace and let them go in 869. From 877 the Danes settled, and Nottingham, a town of about 39 acres, became one of the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw, until Edward of Wessex took it in 918. King Edmund won the Five Boroughs back for the English in 942.

A fort at the bridge

919

Between 919 and 924 Edward the Elder had defences and houses built at the south end of Trent Bridge, to guard Nottingham against the Danes. This was the beginning of West Bridgford. In 1086 the Domesday survey recorded it as part of the manor of Clifton.

The medieval bridge, 1156–1683

Medieval bridges often had a chapel on or beside them, where a priest said mass for travellers and for the bridge's benefactors. At the Reformation the Acts of 1545 and 1547 that abolished chantries closed them.

Trent Bridge

1156

In 1156 a stone bridge of more than 20 arches was begun here. It was known as the Hethbeth bridge, and two of its arches survive on the south bank. The present iron bridge nearby was designed by Marriott Ogle Tarbotton, built between 1868 and 1871, and widened to 80 feet in 1924–26.

The old bridge in flood, 1683

1683

The medieval bridge had a chapel of St James at one end and was kept by a religious house, until on 21 February 1551 a royal charter passed its repair to the Corporation, with lands to pay for it, the Bridge Estate. Floods damaged it again and again, and in 1683 they swept away its northern half. Rebuilt with fifteen arches, 538 ft long in all, it stood until its foundations grew unsafe, and in the 1860s a new bridge was begun.

From ferries to bridges, 17th century–1906

From 1848 towns could set up boards of health, to look after their sewers, streets and water; the first were a response to cholera. It was the age of the borough engineer.

Ferry Inn

17th century

The inn was made from two houses and an outbuilding of the late 17th century, and is listed Grade II. Wilford stayed a village until the later 19th century. With woods around it and riverside amenities such as the Ferry Inn, it attracted many visitors from Nottingham. The Notts County goalkeeper Albert Iremonger was its landlord until his death in 1958.

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton

Trent Bridge

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton MICE, FGS, FRMS, was born in Leeds on 6 December 1834 and died in Nottingham on 6 March 1887. He was Borough Engineer for Nottingham from 1859.

Wikipedia: Marriott Ogle Tarbotton

Marriott Ogle Tarbotton was Nottingham's Borough Engineer from 1859. He designed Trent Bridge, planned the town's underground sewers, culverted the River Leen and built the viaduct over the railway on Carrington Street in 1866.

Wilford Toll Bridge

1870

For some 400 years a ferry crossed the Trent here, until a temporary wooden footbridge replaced it in 1864. On 16 June 1870 the toll bridge opened, and pedestrians paid a halfpenny to cross; it is still known locally as the Halfpenny Bridge. It was closed to traffic in 1974, and in 1980 its centre span was replaced by a narrower footbridge. In 2014–15 it was widened again to carry the trams of Nottingham Express Transit, and it is now a tram, pedestrian and cycle bridge.

Wikipedia: Wilford Toll Bridge

1906

Opened in 1906 to carry water mains across the Trent to the reservoir on Wilford Hill, with a footway over them; it was designed by Arthur Brown of Elliott & Brown. It was restored in 1983 and again in 2008–2010.

The river tamed, 1898–1971

In March 1947 snow that had lain all winter thawed fast over frozen ground and ran straight into the rivers; more than 100,000 properties across the country were flooded.

Victoria Embankment

1898

Between 1898 and 1901 a mile and a quarter of flood defences was built along the Trent under the borough engineer Arthur Brown. Part of it was later laid out as Memorial Gardens to the dead of the First World War.

The 1947 flood

built in 1868

In 1947 the Trent flooded 3,000 properties, 86 factories and 28 miles of road in Nottingham, and the city's first flood defence was built that year. Photographs of West Bridgford under water are in Inspire Picture Archive. A new defence along 27 km of the river, from Sawley to Colwick, was finished in 2012.

Clifton Bridge

1958

In 1958 a bridge opened here to take traffic off Trent Bridge. It was then the longest pre-stressed concrete bridge in the country, and a second bridge beside it, opened in 1972, made the crossing a dual carriageway.
