The square where the two medieval towns met: its market and Goose Fair, St Peter's, the frame-knitting trade, old inns, the Exchange and the Council House that replaced it.

Old Market Square

11th century

One of the largest paved squares in the United Kingdom. The two towns were once divided by a wall across it, whose line a stainless steel channel now marks; Goose Fair was held here until 1928.

Goose Fair

A royal charter of 1284 refers to Nottingham's fairs. For centuries Goose Fair was held in the Old Market Square, until in 1928, having outgrown the city centre, it moved to the Forest Recreation Ground. It was cancelled for the Great Plague in 1646 and during the World Wars.

The frame-knitting town

1728

After William Lee died in about 1614 the stocking frame spread slowly: in 1644 London had some 400 to 500 frames and Nottinghamshire about 150. In 1728 Nottingham's magistrates refused the authority of the London Company of Framework Knitters, and the town became a chief centre of the trade. England had some 14,000 frames by 1750, and in 1758 Jedediah Strutt's "Derby rib" let them knit ribbing. Short of yarn, the knitters looked for ways to spin cotton here.

The cheese riot, 1766

1766

After a poor harvest in 1766, food was dear and people feared shortages. At Goose Fair that October, when Lincolnshire merchants began carting away cheese they had bought, the townspeople tried to stop them; the fair turned to a riot, shops, a warehouse and a boat were looted, and wheels of cheese were rolled down the streets. The mayor could not restore order, and soldiers fired on the crowd, killing William Eggleston, a farmer guarding his own goods. It took several days to bring the town back to order.

Malt Cross

1877

The site was once an inn called the Roebuck. In 1877 a music hall was built here, to designs credited to Edwin Hill, under a high arched glass roof on laminated wooden arches. It took its name from a monument that once stood in the market square, and it is one of the few Victorian music halls to survive.

The Exchange

1724

In 1724–26 the town's Exchange was built in the Market Place to a design by the mayor, Marmaduke Pennell. It held the Corporation's offices, and its Great Hall was used for concerts, elections and balls. It was demolished in 1926 to make way for the Council House.

Sturge and Walter, 1842

1842

When George Larpent resigned his Nottingham seat, Joseph Sturge stood at the by-election: a reformer whose Complete Suffrage Union, a more middle-class rival to the Chartists' own association, campaigned for votes for all men. He lost, and the Conservative John Walter was elected on 4 August 1842; Walter was unseated on petition the next year, and the Liberal Thomas Gisborne won the seat in April 1843. Five years later Nottingham elected the Chartist leader Feargus O'Connor. Where the poll was held is not known.

Three seats for Nottingham, 1885

1885

From 1283 the borough of Nottingham sent two members to Parliament. In the mid-18th century the great landowners, the Duke of Newcastle for the Whigs and Lord Middleton for the Tories, held sway, and the town usually returned one of each. In 1885 the old two-member seat was abolished, and the city was divided into three seats of one member each: Nottingham East, South and West.

Council House

1929

The Council House, designed by Thomas Cecil Howitt, opened in 1929 on the site of the old Exchange at the head of the Old Market Square. The sculptor Joseph Else named the two stone lions on its steps Agamemnon and Menelaus. Little John, the hour bell in its dome, weighs over 10 tonnes, and its strike can be heard 7 miles away.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Council House

Statue of Brian Clough

1975 · made 2008

Brian Clough managed Nottingham Forest from January 1975 to May 1993. Under him the club won the League in 1977–78 and the European Cup in 1979 and 1980. Les Johnson's statue of him, at the corner of King Street and Queen Street, was unveiled by his widow Barbara on 6 November 2008.

Houses, hotels and halls

Around the square: a Georgian house with its library, theatres, a hotel, concert and music halls, and a skating rink that became Rock City.

Bromley House

1752

A Georgian town house was built here in 1752. The Nottingham Subscription Library, founded in 1816, moved in during 1821. In 1836 a meridian line was laid so that the library's longcase clock could keep Nottingham time, 4 minutes and 33 seconds behind Greenwich, and it still does. In 1841 Alfred Barber opened the first photographic studio in the Midlands in its attics.

Wikipedia: Bromley House Library

Theatre Royal

1865

Built for the lace manufacturers John and William Lambert, who wanted a prestigious theatre for the town, to designs by Charles J. Phipps; its portico of six Corinthian columns closes the view up Market Street. It opened on 25 September 1865 with Sheridan's The School for Scandal, and in 1897 Frank Matcham remodelled its auditorium.

Wikipedia: Theatre Royal, Nottingham

The Black Boy Hotel

A hotel on Long Row, rebuilt in 1886–88 by Watson Fothergill and enlarged around 1900. It was demolished in 1970, and a new shop building, first Littlewoods, went up in its place.

Royal Concert Hall

1898 · made 1982

In 1898 Frank Matcham designed and built the Empire Palace of Varieties here. It closed for the last time in 1958 and was demolished for road-widening in 1969. On 27 November 1982 the Royal Concert Hall, designed by the Renton Howard Wood Levin Partnership, opened on the site, with Elton John as its first performer.

Albert Hall

1909

The first Albert Hall, a temperance hall by Watson Fothergill, opened in 1876 and from 1902 was a Wesleyan Methodist mission; it burnt down on 22 April 1906. Albert Edward Lambert designed the present hall, dedicated in 1909, and it stayed a Methodist mission until 1982. It is now a conference and concert venue.

Wikipedia: Albert Hall, Nottingham

Nottingham Playhouse

1948 · made 1963

The Playhouse began in 1948 as a repertory theatre in a former cinema on Goldsmith Street. Its present building, a modernist theatre designed by Peter Moro, opened here in 1963. In 2001 Anish Kapoor's Sky Mirror was put up between the theatre and Wellington Circus, and in 2004 the building was restored with a Heritage Lottery Fund grant.

Wikipedia: Nottingham Playhouse

Rock City

The building opened in 1876 as the Alexandra Skating Rink. Rock City opened in December 1980, with the Undertones playing the first gig, and holds 2,000 people.
