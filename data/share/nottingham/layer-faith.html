Faith

Nottingham's churches and chapels record a long religious history: the medieval parishes, a priory dissolved at the Reformation, and the growth of Nonconformity and Methodism in the industrial town. William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army, was born here.

Before the churches, Roman times–c. 1000

Christianity came to the Anglo-Saxons with the Gregorian mission, which landed in Kent in 597. Mercia, the kingdom that took in Nottingham, turned to it after its pagan king Penda died in 655.

The temple on Red Hill

Roman times

A Roman temple stood on Red Hill, above the meeting of the Soar and the Trent, perhaps with a small town beside it, and at least three curse tablets have been found at the site. Remains of a Roman villa and other buildings, and Roman pottery, have also been found in Ratcliffe.

Stapleford Cross

c. 1000

The Anglo-Saxon cross in St Helen's churchyard dates from around AD 1000, though some put it as early as the 8th century. Its shaft is carved with interlace and a winged figure with a spear, perhaps an archangel or St Luke. Its head was lost in the 18th century; after several moves it came to the churchyard in 1928. Pevsner called it by far the most important pre-Conquest monument in Nottinghamshire, and it is listed Grade I.

Wikipedia: Stapleford Cross

Domesday and the Norman churches, 1086–1200

After the Conquest of 1066 the Normans brought the Romanesque style, with its round arches, to England. They rebuilt every English cathedral and almost every monastic church, and built hundreds of new parish churches.

St Mary de la Roche

12th century

A chapel was cut into the sandstone here, about a quarter of a mile west of the castle, and from at least the late 12th century two monks from Lenton kept it. In 1474 it passed to the King and Gervase Clifton in exchange for the chapel at Tickhill Castle. Puritans wrecked much of it in the Civil War, calling the caves "Papist Holes", and in the mid-19th century they were made into refreshment rooms. The site is now behind flats on Castle Boulevard.

The Byron vault

12th century

St Mary Magdalene's stands on the site of a Saxon church. Its tower was built in stages between the 12th and 14th centuries, and its porch in 1320. Most of the Lords Byron lie in the family vault here, among them the poet Lord Byron, and his daughter Ada, Countess of Lovelace, rests there too. Byron died in Greece on 19 April 1824; Westminster Abbey refused to bury him, and after he lay in state in London he was brought here. The church is listed Grade II*.

The Reformation, 1535–1539

When the Pope refused to end Henry VIII's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, Parliament abolished the Pope's authority in England and made the king supreme head of the Church of England. The break with Rome gave the Crown control of the Church, and then of its monasteries.

Beauvale Priory

1535 · 1343

Nicholas de Cantelupe founded this Carthusian priory in 1343. Its prior, Robert Lawrence, would not accept Henry VIII as head of the Church and was executed at Tyburn in 1535; he was made a saint in 1970. The priory was surrendered on 18 July 1539, and substantial remains of its buildings survive.

Wikipedia: Beauvale Priory

Lenton Priory

1538

William Peverel founded a priory of Cluniac monks at Lenton in about 1102–08, and richly endowed it. In February 1538 its last prior, Nicholas Heath, and many of his monks were thrown into prison, accused of high treason. The prior, eight monks and four Lenton labourers were executed, in Nottingham and in front of the priory, and the priory was dissolved that year. Little is left: the Priory Church of St Anthony is thought to keep part of its hospital chapel.

Wikipedia: Lenton Priory

Shelford's church and priory

1539 · 1310

Ralph Haunselyn founded an Augustinian priory at Shelford around 1160–80, and by 1310–11 the church belonged to it. After Henry VIII dissolved the monasteries, the Stanhopes acquired the priory, in 1537, and with it the church. Lady Anne Stanhope's tomb, by Gabriel Royle, was put up after her death in 1588, and the family vault is dated 1677. In 1877–78 Lord Carnarvon paid for Ewan Christian to restore the church.

War and rebuilding, 1643–1779

After the Civil War and the Restoration, the Act of Uniformity of 1662 required every minister to be ordained by a bishop and to follow the Book of Common Prayer. Over 2,000 clergymen refused and were expelled from their churches: the Great Ejection.

St Nicholas' Church

1643 · 1678

A church of St Nicholas stood here from the 11th or 12th century. In the Civil War, Royalists took its tower and fired on the castle's garrison, so in 1643 the castle's governor, Colonel John Hutchinson, had it destroyed. For 28 years there was no church here; the brick church standing today was begun in 1671 and finished in 1678.

Wikipedia: St Nicholas Church, Nottingham

Chapels and Methodism, 1690–1839

The Toleration Act received royal assent on 24 May 1689. It let Protestant dissenters, such as Baptists, Congregationalists and Presbyterians, worship in their own meeting houses, once these were registered and their preachers licensed. It did not apply to Roman Catholics.

John Wesley

Wesley Place Methodist Chapel, Hockley Methodist Chapel

John Wesley was an English cleric, theologian, and evangelist who was a principal leader of a revival movement within the Church of England known as Methodism. The societies he founded became the dominant form of the ongoing independent Methodist movement. Educated at Charterhouse and Christ Church, Oxford, Wesley was elected a fellow of Lincoln College, Oxford, in 1726 and ordained as an Anglican priest two years later.

Halifax Place Wesleyan Chapel

1798

Methodists met in Nottingham from about 1740, in rooms here and there, and in 1782 moved to Hockley Chapel on Goose Gate. When the New Connexion split from the Wesleyans in 1797 and took that chapel, the Wesleyans bought land in Halifax Place and opened a chapel for 1,600 on 2 December 1798. Its successor of 1847 had the highest ceiling of any Wesleyan chapel in England; it closed in 1930 and was pulled down in 1966.

Churches for new districts, 1812–1903

Parliament's Church Building Act of 1818 gave £1 million for new churches, mainly in growing industrial towns where people had outrun the churches, and a second grant of £500,000 followed in 1824.

Missions and the Salvation Army, 1829–1909

On one Sunday in 1851 the census counted everyone at worship across England and Wales: 10,896,066 attendances, out of a population of 17,927,609. Fewer than half were at the Church of England, and the count was never repeated.

William Booth

William Booth's birthplace, Plaque to William Booth

William Booth was an English Methodist preacher who, along with his wife, Catherine, founded the Salvation Army and became its first General. This Christian movement, founded in 1865, has a quasi-military structure and government and has spread from London to many parts of the world. It is one of the largest distributors of humanitarian aid.

Wikipedia: William Booth

William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army, was born in this house in Notintone Place, Sneinton. It is now the William Booth Birthplace Museum, telling the story of Booth, his wife Catherine and their family.

Albert Hall

1909

The first Albert Hall, a temperance hall by Watson Fothergill, opened in 1876 and from 1902 was a Wesleyan Methodist mission; it burnt down on 22 April 1906. Albert Edward Lambert designed the present hall, dedicated in 1909, and it stayed a Methodist mission until 1982. It is now a conference and concert venue.
