Before the English, c. 1000 BC–AD 400

The Roman road called the Fosse Way was built in the first and second centuries AD, from Exeter in the south-west to Lincoln in the north-east, and crossed this county with a stopping place at Castle Hill near Bingham.

Roman Broxtowe

1st century

Workmen laying the sewers and roads of the new Broxtowe council estate found Roman coins and pottery. That was in 1937. Later finds included a mount from the scabbard of a Roman sword, and in 1964 a ditch gave up pottery and pieces of a lava quern of the 1st century AD. The site has been taken for an early Roman fort of about AD 50 to 75. Another view is that it was a British hut settlement, not a fort.

Wikipedia: Broxtowe, Nottingham

Roman Calverton

Roman times

Aerial photographs show traces of two Roman marching camps in a field north-east of the Oxton Road and Whinbush Lane crossroads: a smaller one of four acres inside a larger one of about twenty-six acres. Nothing shows above ground, and the site is a scheduled monument. Camps like these were dug by an army unit for an overnight stop. Two very similar hoards of Roman coins were later found in the village, less than three hundred yards apart: in June 1959, while foundations were dug for Manor Park Infants' School on Collyer Road, and in about April 1960, while a house was built in Crookdole Lane. The first was buried in an earthenware pot.

Wikipedia: Calverton, Nottinghamshire

The English settle, 6th–10th centuries

Most places in England still bear Old English names, and many of them hold the names of the settlers who founded them. Nottingham is one: Snotingaham, the homestead of the Snotingas, the people of a man called Snot.

Stapleford Cross

c. 1000

The Anglo-Saxon cross in St Helen's churchyard dates from around AD 1000, though some put it as early as the 8th century. Its shaft is carved with interlace and a winged figure with a spear, perhaps an archangel or St Luke. Its head was lost in the 18th century; after several moves it came to the churchyard in 1928. Pevsner called it by far the most important pre-Conquest monument in Nottinghamshire, and it is listed Grade I.

Wikipedia: Stapleford Cross

The Danes, 868–924

From 877 Danish armies settled the East Midlands, each round a fortified town: Derby, Leicester, Lincoln, Nottingham and Stamford, the Five Boroughs. They left names of their own, such as -by for a village and -thorpe for an outlying farm, and in Nottingham the streets called -gate, from the Old Norse gata, a road: Barker Gate, Castle Gate, Fisher Gate.

The Danes in Nottingham, 868

868

In 868 a Viking army under Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan Ragnarsson took Nottingham and wintered here. Burgred of Mercia and his West Saxon allies laid siege, but made peace and let them go in 869. From 877 the Danes settled, and Nottingham, a town of about 39 acres, became one of the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw, until Edward of Wessex took it in 918. King Edmund won the Five Boroughs back for the English in 942.

Domesday Book, 1086

In 1085 the king sent his agents to survey every shire in England, to record what was owed to him and who now held the land. Its name came from the Last Judgement: from its record, as from that, there was no appeal.

12th century

Domesday Book calls the village Aitone. The manor of Wilford

1280

Before the Conquest the manor of Wilford belonged to Gode the Countess. In Domesday Book the settlement is named Willesforde, with a fishery, a priest and 23 sokemen. Clifton and Wilford, Peverel lands, passed to the Crown early in Henry II's reign, and the king granted them to Gerbode de Escalt; in Richard I's time they passed to the de Rodes family. Before 1280 Gervase de Clifton obtained both manors from Sir Gerard de Rodes for £30 a year, and a charter of 1280 confirmed the grant. In 1853 Sir J. G. J. Clifton was lord of the manor, owned all its land but the church's glebe, and was patron of the church; in 1870–72 the manor still belonged to Sir R. J. Clifton.

Wikipedia: Wilford

Names given since, 1285–1807

After the Norman Conquest some places gained a second part to their name, the name of the family that now held them, but England's place-names have changed little since the early Norman period. The new names went to streets, districts and springs.

Walker Gate

1285

From about 1285 the area was known as Walker Gate, after the "walking", or treading, of cloth to thicken it. Nottingham's wool trade mattered: between 1343 and 1345 the price of wool in its market was the standard for all of England.

St Ann's Well

1301

The district takes its name from a spring once thought to heal. It was recorded in 1301 as the Brodewell, and was also called Robin Hood's Well; in 1500 monks built a chapel beside it and dedicated it to St Ann. A spired cover put over it in 1856 was pulled down in 1887 for the railway, and the site was later built over by the Gardeners pub.

The name goes back at least to the 14th century. Thomas Mapurley was under-sheriff of Nottinghamshire from about 1387 and mayor of Nottingham in 1402–03. The high ground to the north-east was long known as Mapperley Plains.
