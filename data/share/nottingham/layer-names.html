Tilchestune

1086

Ilkeston was probably founded in the 6th century, and is said to take its name from a founder called Elch. Domesday Book, in 1086, calls it Tilchestune, held mostly by Gilbert de Ghent. Queen Victoria made it a borough in 1887.

Addensburgh

In Saxon times the village was known as Addensburgh, and a ferry crossed the Trent here from before 1774. In 1966 a hoard of Roman coins was found on a footpath near the railway and Barrett Lane.

The Danes in Nottingham, 868

868

In 868 a Viking army under Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan Ragnarsson took Nottingham and wintered here. Burgred of Mercia and his West Saxon allies laid siege, but made peace and let them go in 869. From 877 the Danes settled, and Nottingham, a town of about 39 acres, became one of the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw, until Edward of Wessex took it in 918. King Edmund won the Five Boroughs back for the English in 942.

Wikipedia: Five Boroughs of the Danelaw

A fort at the bridge

919

Between 919 and 924 Edward the Elder had defences and houses built at the south end of Trent Bridge, to guard Nottingham against the Danes. This was the beginning of West Bridgford. In 1086 the Domesday survey recorded it as part of the manor of Clifton.

Stapleford Cross

c. 1000

The Anglo-Saxon cross in St Helen's churchyard dates from around AD 1000, though some put it as early as the 8th century. Its shaft is carved with interlace and a winged figure with a spear, perhaps an archangel or St Luke. Its head was lost in the 18th century; after several moves it came to the churchyard in 1928. Pevsner called it by far the most important pre-Conquest monument in Nottinghamshire, and it is listed Grade I.

Wikipedia: Stapleford Cross

In Domesday Book

In 1086 Domesday Book recorded many of the villages round Nottingham, some under names we would barely know.

Ernehale

1086

Domesday Book, in 1086, calls the village Ernehale. A history of Arnold written in 1913 traces the name back to its Anglo-Saxon settlers, worn down over the centuries into Arnold.

St Laurence's, Long Eaton

12th century

Domesday Book calls the village Aitone. Its church of St Laurence stands east of the Market Place: local tradition dates parts of it to the 11th century and King Cnut, but its oldest parts may be from after the Conquest, in the 12th century. It was a daughter church of Sawley until the 19th century. It is listed Grade II*.

The manor of Wilford

1280

Before the Conquest the manor of Wilford belonged to Gode the Countess. In Domesday Book the settlement is named Willesforde, with a fishery, a priest and 23 sokemen. Clifton and Wilford, Peverel lands, passed to the Crown early in Henry II's reign, and the king granted them to Gerbode de Escalt; in Richard I's time they passed to the de Rodes family. Before 1280 Gervase de Clifton obtained both manors from Sir Gerard de Rodes for £30 a year, and a charter of 1280 confirmed the grant. In 1853 Sir J. G. J. Clifton was lord of the manor, owned all its land but the church's glebe, and was patron of the church; in 1870–72 the manor still belonged to Sir R. J. Clifton.

Wikipedia: Wilford

Names given since

Other names came later, from the cloth trade, a healing spring, a local official or a legend.

Walker Gate

1285

From about 1285 the area was known as Walker Gate, after the "walking", or treading, of cloth to thicken it. Nottingham's wool trade mattered: between 1343 and 1345 the price of wool in its market was the standard for all of England.

Wikipedia: Hockley, Nottingham

St Ann's Well

1301

The district takes its name from a spring once thought to heal. It was recorded in 1301 as the Brodewell, and was also called Robin Hood's Well; in 1500 monks built a chapel beside it and dedicated it to St Ann. A spired cover put over it in 1856 was pulled down in 1887 for the railway, and the site was later built over by the Gardeners pub.

Wikipedia: St Ann's, Nottingham

Thomas Mapurley

1387

The name goes back at least to the 14th century. Thomas Mapurley was under-sheriff of Nottinghamshire from about 1387 and mayor of Nottingham in 1402–03. The high ground to the north-east was long known as Mapperley Plains.

Barton in the Beans

The village's older name was Barton in the Beans, after the beans grown here; fabis is Latin for beans. A ferry crossed the Trent from here to Attenborough from before 1774. Today Fairham, a new district, is being built in the parish beside Clifton.

Gylden Broc

Giltbrook's name is thought to come from the Old English Gylden Broc, the golden brook, after the stream that runs down from the fields north of where IKEA now stands. A retail park was built beside IKEA in 2008.

Goat home to Gotham City

1807

Gotham means "goat home" in Old English. Washington Irving gave the name "Gotham" to New York in his Salmagundi Papers of 1807, and Bill Finger in turn named Batman's city Gotham City.

Wikipedia: Gotham, Nottinghamshire
