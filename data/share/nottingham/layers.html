The map of Nottingham

Stories of Nottingham

  • Castle rock 23 places
    The sandstone rock at the western end of the old town has been the site of Nottingham's castle since 1068. Kings held court here, the Civil War began below it, and the mansion that replaced the castle was burned in 1831 and later became the city's museum. Old inns and caves lie at its foot.
  • Market Square 23 places
    The Old Market Square was the centre of Nottingham for centuries. It lay where the town's Saxon and Norman settlements met, and was for long the site of the town's markets, fairs and government. The surrounding streets hold some of the city's oldest inns and many of its theatres and halls.
  • Lace Market 20 places
    The hill east of the Market Square was the site of the Saxon town, taken by the Danes in 868 and centred on St Mary's Church. In the 19th century lace merchants built their warehouses and showrooms here, giving the district its name. Below it lay the marshy ground of Broad Marsh.
  • North of the centre 23 places
    North of the old town lay open fields and common land until the 19th century. As Nottingham expanded, the area was used for public parks and cemeteries, a Catholic cathedral, a college and a great railway station. It is now part of the city centre.
  • Sneinton 8 places
    Sneinton lies on the hill east of the city. Its windmill belonged to the family of the mathematician George Green, William Booth was born here in 1829, and the city's wholesale market moved here in the 1930s. Cave dwellings were once dug into its sandstone ridge.
  • St Ann's 8 places
    St Ann's takes its name from a medieval well once thought to heal. Its hillside allotments are among the oldest in England, and after enclosure in 1845 the fields were covered with dense terraced housing, much of which was cleared and rebuilt around 1970.
  • The Meadows 11 places
    The Meadows, the low ground between the city and the Trent, was built over with terraced housing before the First World War. The area was protected by the Victoria Embankment, is home to Notts County's ground, and was largely rebuilt in the 1970s.
  • Hockley 7 places
    Hockley lies below the Lace Market, where Nottingham's cloth workers gave the medieval street Walker Gate its name. In the 18th century Arkwright built his first cotton mill here, Methodists opened a chapel, and in 1849 John Boot opened his shop on Goose Gate. More recently it has become known for its independent cinemas.
  • The Park Estate 9 places
    The Park began as the castle's deer park. From the 1820s the Dukes of Newcastle developed it as a private estate of villas for the well-to-do, many designed by T. C. Hine, and its streets are still lit by gas.
  • Castle Marina 4 places
    The Nottingham Canal, opened in 1796, brought coal and goods into the town, and wharves and warehouses grew up along it west of the centre. When the canal trade declined, the old railway sidings beside it were dug out in the 1980s for a marina.
  • Wilford 21 places
    Wilford is a village on the south bank of the Trent, linked to Nottingham for centuries by ferry and, from 1870, by a toll bridge. Its church, manor and inn record a long rural history; in the late 19th century it became a suburb.
  • West Bridgford 21 places
    West Bridgford, across the Trent from the city, grew from a small village into a large suburb from the late 19th century. It is the home of Trent Bridge cricket ground, of Nottingham Forest's City Ground, and of the county council.
  • Clifton 14 places
    For centuries Clifton was the village of the Clifton family, who held the manor from the 13th century. After the Second World War the council built a very large housing estate beside it, changing the area completely, while the old village survived on its hill above the Trent.
  • Barton in Fabis 4 places
    Barton in Fabis is a small farming village on the south bank of the Trent. Its name records the beans once grown in its fields, and its medieval church and 17th-century dovecote reflect a long agricultural history, with a ferry linking it across the river.
  • Thrumpton 6 places
    Thrumpton is a small estate village on the Trent. Its hall was built by the Pigot family in the early 17th century, and later owners built much of the village. The estate has passed through several families, including the Byrons.
  • Ruddington 6 places
    Ruddington was a village of framework knitters south of the city, and its workshops are now a museum. Nearby stood the medieval church of Flawford, demolished in 1779, and in the 20th century a railway station and an ammunition depot were built on the village's edge.
  • Gotham 5 places
    Gotham is best known for the tales of its 'Wise Men', the fools of Gotham, already told in the 15th century. Its name later gave New York the nickname 'Gotham'. The village also had its medieval church and, from the late 19th century, gypsum mining.
  • Ratcliffe-on-Soar 3 places
    Ratcliffe-on-Soar is a small village by the River Soar, near the southern edge of the county. It was overshadowed from 1968 by its coal-fired power station, which in 2024 became the last in Britain to close. A main-line station was built nearby in 2008.
  • Holme Pierrepont 5 places
    Holme Pierrepont, on the flat land along the Trent east of the city, was held by the Pierrepont family from the 13th century. Their Tudor brick hall and family church still stand. In the 20th century plans for a large power station here were dropped, and the gravel workings became the National Watersports Centre.
  • Shelford 3 places
    Shelford, on the Trent, was the site of an Augustinian priory. After the Dissolution the Stanhope family acquired the land, and their house was garrisoned for the King in the Civil War until Parliament's forces stormed it in 1645.
  • Tollerton 3 places
    Tollerton is a village south-east of the city with a 12th-century church. Its hall became a Catholic seminary in 1948, and the airfield nearby, opened in 1930, was one of the first municipal aerodromes in Britain.
  • Cotgrave 3 places
    Cotgrave has a long history, from an Anglo-Saxon burial ground and a 12th-century church to its transformation in the 1960s, when a new colliery brought mining families from closed pits and the village grew several times over.
  • Radford 5 places
    Radford was a village west of Nottingham whose church belonged to Lenton Priory. In the 19th century it became one of the city's main industrial districts, home to Raleigh's bicycle works and Player's tobacco factories, and the setting of Alan Sillitoe's Saturday Night and Sunday Morning.
  • Lenton 10 places
    Lenton was dominated for over four centuries by its Cluniac priory, founded in about 1102 and dissolved in 1538. Industrial growth came in the 19th century, with Raleigh's bicycle works, and in the 20th the University of Nottingham was built at Highfields, where MRI was later developed.
  • Wollaton 6 places
    Wollaton was the village of the Willoughby family, who also worked coal here. Their Elizabethan hall, built in the 1580s, still dominates the park, and as early as 1603–04 a wooden wagonway carried coal from their pits. The last colliery here closed in 1965.
  • Beeston 4 places
    Beeston grew from a village into an industrial town in the 19th and 20th centuries. Its silk mill was burned in the Reform riots of 1831, Thomas Humber built his bicycle works here around 1878, and in the 1930s Boots built its large factories on the town's edge.
  • Chilwell 6 places
    Chilwell began as a hamlet on the road to Ashby-de-la-Zouch, where Roman finds show earlier settlement. It grew into a suburb in the 20th century, and in the First World War it was the site of the National Shell Filling Factory, where an explosion killed 134 people in 1918.
  • Giltbrook 3 places
    Giltbrook, named after the stream that runs through it, lies in the Erewash valley. In 1817 the Pentrich rebels, marching on Nottingham, were dispersed nearby, and in the 1870s the Great Northern Railway built its long viaduct, the Forty Bridges, across the valley.
  • Eastwood 4 places
    Eastwood was a mining town on the Nottinghamshire–Derbyshire border. In 1832 local coal owners met at the Sun Inn to plan a railway, which became part of the Midland Railway, and in 1885 D. H. Lawrence was born here, the son of a miner.
  • Bennerley 2 places
    In the Erewash valley, on the border of Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire, coal and iron were worked side by side in the 19th century. The ironworks at Bennerley and the wrought-iron viaduct built in 1876–77 belong to that industrial landscape, much of it undermined by coal workings.
  • Attenborough 4 places
    Attenborough is an old village on the Trent, known in Saxon times as Addensburgh. The Civil War general Henry Ireton came from here, and its churchyard holds the memorial to those killed in the Chilwell explosion of 1918. Gravel digging in the 20th century left the lakes of the nature reserve.
  • Stapleford 4 places
    Stapleford is an old village west of the city with an Anglo-Saxon cross of about AD 1000 in its churchyard. On the hill nearby stands the Hemlock Stone, a natural sandstone pillar surrounded by legend.
  • Arnold 8 places
    Arnold was a Domesday village north of Nottingham that grew into a town of hosiery and cotton. Its mill owners and framework knitters were part of the county's textile industry, and in 1811 the Luddite frame-breaking began here. The painter Richard Parkes Bonington and the water engineer Thomas Hawksley were both born in Arnold.
  • Calverton 4 places
    Calverton has two claims to a place in history. William Lee, who invented the stocking frame in 1589, came from here, and in 1952 a new colliery opened that quadrupled the village's population within a decade.
  • Gedling 4 places
    Gedling was a farming village east of the city, with a 13th-century church. From 1899 its colliery was the main local employer for almost a century; the railway station that served it closed in 1960, and the colliery site has since become a country park.
  • Bestwood 4 places
    Bestwood began as a royal hunting park, recorded from 1286. In the 19th century it belonged to the Dukes of St Albans, who built a new lodge, and in the 1870s and 1880s its colliery and ironworks brought industry and a new village to the old parkland.
  • Papplewick 3 places
    Papplewick lies on the River Leen north of Nottingham. In the late 18th century cotton mills were built along the river and Papplewick Hall was built nearby, and in the 1880s a pumping station was built here to raise drinking water for the growing city.
  • Hucknall 7 places
    Hucknall was a village of framework knitters that became a mining town after its collieries opened in the 1860s. Lord Byron is buried in its parish church. From 1927 Rolls-Royce tested aero engines at its airfield, and in 2004 the tram arrived.
  • Basford 7 places
    Basford, recorded in Domesday Book, lay along the River Leen. In the 19th century the river's clean water drew bleachers, and the village became an industrial suburb with breweries, a sauce factory and a soap works. It was also home to the poet Philip James Bailey and the motorcycle maker George Brough.
  • Bulwell 8 places
    Bulwell, recorded in Domesday Book, was a market town on the River Leen with its own trades: limestone quarrying, glove-making and bleaching. Its people defended their common land, the Bogs, when the lord of the manor tried to enclose it in 1872.
  • Sherwood 4 places
    Sherwood grew up along Mansfield Road, the old main road north from Nottingham. A Georgian house is its oldest building, and in the 19th century the area was laid out for housing, reached by tram from 1900.
  • Mapperley 7 places
    Mapperley takes its name from Thomas Mapurley, a 14th-century official. The high ground north-east of the town was the estate of the Wright family, and in the 19th century it was chosen for hospitals and asylums. Its clay also supplied some of the area's largest brickworks.
  • Carlton 6 places
    Carlton was a small township of farmers and framework knitters east of Nottingham. In the 19th century the textile trade and the railway made it a growing suburb, with a new church built by the Earl of Carnarvon and brickworks on the hill.
  • People 244 places
    People connected with buildings on the map: who designed them, who lived, worked or played in them, and who is remembered on their walls. Entries come from commemorative plaques (Open Plaques), architects recorded in Wikidata, and hand-written entries checked against Wikipedia and Historic England.
  • Long Eaton 6 places
    Long Eaton, recorded in Domesday Book, remained a village until the railway arrived in 1839. It then grew rapidly, with a large railway yard nearby and, from the 1850s, lace mills, and its new prosperity can be seen in its Edwardian and Art Nouveau buildings.
  • Ilkeston 5 places
    Ilkeston, on a hill above the Erewash, received a market charter from Henry III in 1252 and still holds its charter fair. The town grew with the coal and iron industries of the Erewash valley, especially the Stanton ironworks. It lost its railway stations in the 20th century and regained one in 2017.
  • Broxtowe 1 places
    Broxtowe is a council estate in the north-west of the city, in Aspley ward, perhaps named after Broxtowe Hall, which was pulled down in 1937. Building the estate brought much older finds to light.
  • Kimberley and Watnall 1 places
    Kimberley, north-west of the city, grew as a centre of coal mining, brewing and hosiery. In the Second World War neighbouring Watnall held the headquarters of the RAF's fighter group for the Midlands.
  • Linby 2 places
    Linby is a village north of Hucknall, with an old stone cross at each end, and the remains of Annesley Hall's south lodge gates nearby.
  • Bingham 1 places
    Bingham is a market town east of Nottingham. A field at its eastern end holds earthworks long taken for a lost medieval village.
  • Plumtree 1 places
    Plumtree is a small village south-east of Nottingham. Its church of St Mary is believed to be one of the oldest in the county.
  • Names and beginnings 25 places
    Before anything here was written down, people left their names on the land and their things in the ground.
  • Roman times 8 places
    Before the Romans this was the land of the Corieltauvi, a Celtic tribe of the East Midlands whose capital was at Leicester, and there is little sign that they fought the conquest. No Roman town stood where Nottingham is now: the traces of those centuries lie around it.
  • Castle and Crown 23 places
    After the Norman Conquest William the Conqueror built a castle at Nottingham and gave it into the keeping of William Peverel. For six centuries it was the Crown's; then it was the dukes'; at last it was the town's.
  • The Civil War 11 places
    The war grew out of a long struggle between the monarchy and Parliament for political and religious control. Nottingham was where it began, and a Parliament town through all of it, with Royalist garrisons round it.
  • Frames and lace 23 places
    For three centuries Nottingham lived by its textiles. The hosiery trade settled in the town after 1730, and with its offshoot, lace, it came to dominate the town's economy through the 18th and 19th centuries.
  • Risings and riots 14 places
    For a century and more, Nottingham's people took to the streets again and again. The town lived by hosiery, settled there after 1730, and later by lace, trades in which work was notoriously precarious. Few of those workers had a vote: before 1832 only about one Briton in twenty did. And the town grew so fast and so badly that it came to have the worst slums in England. Each chapter here is one outbreak, set against what lay behind it.
  • Reform and the vote 11 places
    The vote was won in steps: by the better-off in 1832, by part of the urban working class in 1867, by many country workers in 1884, by women over 30 in 1918, and by all women, on the same terms as men, in 1928.
  • Coal 17 places
    Britain's coal output peaked in 1913, at 287 million tonnes; its last deep mines closed in 2015. Nottinghamshire's pits, and the wagonways, canals, railways and power stations that served them, lie between those dates.
  • Railways and roads 27 places
    The canal, the railways, the trams and the airfields each came and grew, and most of them went; some have come back.
  • Made in Nottingham 34 places
    Nottingham became a city in 1897, as part of Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee. By then it made far more than lace, and three of its firms, Boots, Raleigh and Player's, became household names; by the end of the 20th century Raleigh and Player's had moved the bulk of their making elsewhere, while Boots thrived.
  • Markets and fairs 14 places
    After the Normans, Nottingham was two boroughs, the English one round the old settlement and the French one under the castle. The open ground between them was built on as the town grew, and became the Old Market Square. Edward I's charter, approved on 12 February 1284, gave the town its first mayor, with two bailiffs who kept the customs of each borough.
  • The Victorian town 28 places
    In the 19th century Nottingham broke out of its old bounds. In 1877 the borough took in the parishes of Basford, Bulwell, Radford, Sneinton and others round it.
  • Crossing the Trent 17 places
    The Trent is the third-longest river in the United Kingdom, and it once marked the boundary between the north of England and the south. Nottingham grew where it could be crossed.
  • Science and health 25 places
    A library and a hospital paid for by subscribers, a miller who taught himself mathematics, engineers who brought clean water, women who fought their way into medicine, and two discoveries that went round the world.
  • Soldiers and wars 27 places
    Nottingham sent soldiers and sailors to wars across the centuries and raised its own county regiment. The two World Wars also reached the city itself, through the munitions factories and depots, the losses at Chilwell and the bombing of 1941.
  • Statues and memorials 14 places
    Statues and memorials show who a town chose to honour, and when. Nottingham's range from a Chartist leader and Victorian MPs to the city's war dead and, in the 20th and 21st centuries, figures of legend and football, and at last the women of its past.
  • Art Nouveau 6 places
    Art Nouveau, the style of curving lines and plant forms that was at its height around 1900, never shaped Nottingham's large buildings, which stayed Gothic or Baroque. It arrived in the details instead: in shopfronts and the tiles of a shop's side wall, in a station's iron gates, and in the porches of ordinary terraced houses.
  • Lost Nottingham 20 places
    Much of historic Nottingham has gone. Some buildings were cleared for road schemes and shopping centres in the 20th century; others were destroyed in riots or simply outlived their purpose. These stories record what stood on sites that look very different today.
  • Great houses 28 places
    The countryside around Nottingham was dominated for centuries by landed families: the Cliftons, Pierreponts, Willoughbys and others. Their houses and estates shaped the villages around them, and many were sold, divided or put to new uses in the 20th century.
  • Faith 48 places
    Nottingham's churches and chapels record a long religious history: the medieval parishes, a priory dissolved at the Reformation, and the growth of Nonconformity and Methodism in the industrial town. William Booth, who founded the Salvation Army, was born here.
  • Freemasons 3 places
    Freemasons have met in Nottingham since the 1750s: first in the town's inns, later in a hall of their own on Goldsmith Street, which is still the home of the county's lodges.
  • Women's lives 24 places
    Women's lives in Nottingham are less recorded than men's, but they appear throughout its history: as writers, businesswomen, teachers, doctors, nurses and librarians, and as campaigners for the vote.
  • Writers and artists 29 places
    Nottinghamshire has produced or attracted a number of notable writers and artists. Their work often draws on the life of the area, from the Byrons and the Romantic poets to the mining communities of D. H. Lawrence and the working-class Nottingham of Alan Sillitoe.
  • Sport 27 places
    Nottingham has a long sporting history. Trent Bridge became one of England's main cricket grounds, the city is home to two of the oldest football clubs in the world, and its sportsmen and women have included prize-fighters, rowers and ice dancers.
