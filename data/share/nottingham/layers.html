The map of Nottingham

Stories of Nottingham

  • Castle rock 17 places
    The sandstone rock at the west end of the old town: the castle, the passages and caves beneath it, the inns at its foot and the statues in its grounds.
  • Market Square 20 places
    The Old Market Square, where the two medieval towns met, and the streets around it: the town's Exchange and the Council House that replaced it, old inns, a library, theatres and halls.
  • Lace Market 15 places
    The old Saxon town on its hill east of the square, where lace merchants built their warehouses, and the low ground below it towards the canal and the station.
  • North of the centre 17 places
    The hills north of the old town, built over in the 19th century: parks and cemeteries, a cathedral, a college and a railway station.
  • Sneinton 6 places
    The hill east of the city: a windmill, cave dwellings, a church by Thomas Rickman, the old wholesale market and the later laboratory of the Boots team that discovered ibuprofen.
  • St Ann's 8 places
    The hill north-east of the old town: a healing well, the oldest allotments in England, the terraces of New Town, and the estate that replaced them.
  • The Meadows 11 places
    The low ground between the city and the Trent, with two football grounds facing each other across the river, and a flood.
  • Hockley 7 places
    The old Walker Gate below the Lace Market: cloth, chapels and cotton, the first Boots shop, cinemas, and the city's bus depot.
  • The Park Estate 8 places
    The castle's old deer park, built over with Victorian villas and still lit by gas.
  • Castle Marina 4 places
    The Nottingham Canal west of the town: wharves and warehouses on Canal Street, and a marina on old railway sidings.
  • Wilford 21 places
    A village on the south bank of the Trent, reached for centuries by ferry and, since the 1870s, by bridge.
  • West Bridgford 20 places
    South of the river: Test cricket, Nottingham Forest and the county's headquarters.
  • Clifton 13 places
    An old village above the Trent, and next to it one of the largest housing estates ever built by a council.
  • Barton in Fabis 3 places
    A village on the south bank of the Trent, upstream from Clifton, once called Barton in the Beans: its medieval church, a dovecote of 1677, and the new district of Fairham.
  • Thrumpton 6 places
    A small estate village on the Trent: its Jacobean hall and the families who owned it, its church, and the houses built for the estate.
  • Ruddington 5 places
    A village of framework knitters south of the city, the Second World War ammunition depot beyond it, the site of Flawford's lost church, whose name the village church took, and a Victorian banker's hall.
  • Gotham 5 places
    The village of the Wise Men: its church, the name that travelled to New York and Batman, its gypsum, and the day a tornado hit it.
  • Ratcliffe-on-Soar 2 places
    By the Soar, the power station that ended coal power in Britain, and the main-line station beside it.
  • Holme Pierrepont 5 places
    The flat land along the Trent east of the city: seven centuries of Pierreponts, their Tudor brick hall and church, a power station refused, and the gravel pits that became a centre for watersports.
  • Shelford 3 places
    A village on the Trent: its priory church and the Stanhopes, and the Royalist garrison stormed in the Civil War.
  • Tollerton 3 places
    A 12th-century church, a hall that became a seminary, and one of Britain's first municipal airfields.
  • Cotgrave 3 places
    An Anglo-Saxon burial ground, a 12th-century church, and a village that grew several times over when its colliery came.
  • Radford 5 places
    West of the centre: an old church, Raleigh's bicycles and Player's tobacco, and the streets of Saturday Night and Sunday Morning.
  • Lenton 6 places
    A Cluniac priory, dissolved in 1538, and its fair; Raleigh's works on Faraday Road, and the university at Highfields, where the MRI scanner was developed.
  • Wollaton 6 places
    The Willoughbys' village: its church and dovecote, their Elizabethan hall, the wooden wagonway from the Strelley pits, described as the first railway in Britain, and the colliery that outlasted them.
  • Beeston 4 places
    Humber's bicycle works, a silk mill burned in the Reform riots, and Boots' factories.
  • Chilwell 5 places
    A hamlet on the Ashby road that grew into a suburb, and the shell factory of the First World War.
  • Giltbrook 3 places
    Forty Bridges, the lost railway viaduct, and where the Pentrich marchers of 1817 met the soldiers and scattered.
  • Eastwood 3 places
    The mining town where coal owners planned a railway in an inn, and where D. H. Lawrence was born.
  • Bennerley 2 places
    The ironworks and the iron viaduct of the Erewash valley, on ground undermined by coal workings.
  • Attenborough 4 places
    The village by the Trent: its Saxon name, a general of the Civil War, the memorial to the Chilwell dead, and the gravel pits that became a nature reserve.
  • Stapleford 3 places
    An Anglo-Saxon cross in a medieval churchyard, and a sandstone pillar on the hill full of legends.
  • Arnold 8 places
    A Domesday village and its church, a cotton mill and its house, a painter and a water engineer born here, the first Luddites, a brewery and a market.
  • Calverton 3 places
    The village of William Lee, who invented the stocking frame, and of the colliery that grew it fourfold.
  • Gedling 4 places
    A village church with a famous spire, a colliery that became a country park, a closed station and the woods of Gedling House.
  • Bestwood 4 places
    A royal hunting park, the Dukes of St Albans' lodge, and the colliery and ironworks that made a village.
  • Papplewick 3 places
    Cotton mills on the Leen, a Georgian hall, and the pumping station that brought Nottingham its water.
  • Hucknall 5 places
    The town at the north edge of the map: the church where the Byrons lie, its pits, the airfield where Rolls-Royce tested engines, and the end of the first tram line.
  • Basford 7 places
    A Domesday village on the Leen that bleached, brewed and bottled sauce.
  • Bulwell 7 places
    A market town on the Leen: its stone, its covered stalls, its hall, and the Bogs its people claimed.
  • Sherwood 4 places
    A suburb along Mansfield Road: a Georgian house, a tram depot, and a bleacher's grange that became a park.
  • Mapperley 7 places
    The hill north-east of the town: a medieval name, the Wrights' estate, two asylums, a church and the brickworks.
  • Carlton 4 places
    A township of framework knitters that the railway turned into a suburb, with an earl's church and a brickworks on the hill.
  • People 243 places
    People connected with buildings on the map: who designed them, who lived, worked or played in them, and who is remembered on their walls. Entries come from commemorative plaques (Open Plaques), architects recorded in Wikidata, and hand-written entries checked against Wikipedia and Historic England.
  • Long Eaton 6 places
    A Domesday village by the Erewash that the railway and the lace mills turned into a town.
  • Ilkeston 4 places
    A hill town over the Erewash: its charter fair, its ironworks, and the station it waited fifty years for.
  • Names and beginnings 23 places
    How the places on the map began and got their names: Bronze Age and Roman finds, Saxon burials, a cross and a fort, the villages Domesday Book wrote down in 1086, and names given since.
  • Castle and Crown 18 places
    Kings and the castle on the rock: a favourite seized in 1330, Charles I's call to arms in 1642, the Parliamentary governor who held it, the dukes' mansion that replaced it, and the riot that burned it.
  • The Civil War 11 places
    Nottingham in the Civil War: where the King raised his standard, the castle held for Parliament through raid after raid, a church destroyed in the fighting, a Royalist house stormed and burned, and Shelford's church tower held by Royalists led by a woman.
  • Frames and lace 22 places
    How the stocking frame and the lace machine made Nottingham's trade: the inventors, the mills and workshops, the hosiers and lace merchants, and the frame-breakers who fought the machines.
  • Risings and riots 9 places
    When Nottingham rose: a riot over cheese at Goose Fair, the frame-breakers, a march on the town, the Reform riots that burned the castle and a mill, the Chartists on Mapperley Hills, Bulwell's march to save its common, and the riots of 1958.
  • Reform and the vote 11 places
    Who got a say: the Reform riots of 1831, the Chartists' strike and the election of 1842, the Chartist leader the town elected in 1847, an MP and employer, and the women who campaigned for the vote and first used it in 1918.
  • Coal 16 places
    The pits that dug Nottinghamshire's coal, the wagonway, canal and railway that carried it, and the power stations that burned it.
  • Railways and roads 27 places
    How people got about: the railways and their stations, a bridge turned over to cars, the buses and aircraft that came after, and the trams that came back.
  • Made in Nottingham 31 places
    What Nottingham made: Humber's and Raleigh's bicycles and Brough's motorcycles, Player's cigarettes, the medicines of Boots, from Jesse and Florence Boot's shop to ibuprofen, and Paul Smith's clothes.
  • Markets and fairs 14 places
    Where Nottingham traded and made merry: the great square and the village markets, the fairs granted by medieval kings and a riot over cheese, and the shops that came after.
  • The Victorian town 26 places
    The town that grew in the 19th century: cemeteries and parks on the hills to the north, a college, theatres and halls, and the architects whose buildings line its streets.
  • Crossing the Trent 17 places
    Crossing and taming the river: a Roman ford, the ferries, a fort at the bridge, the medieval bridge and its iron successor, the bridges to Wilford and Clifton, the embankment, a flood and the watersports centre.
  • Science and health 20 places
    Nottingham's scientists, engineers, doctors and nurses: clean water and sewers, a self-taught mathematician, medicines and the MRI scanner.
  • Soldiers and wars 18 places
    Soldiers and sailors who lived here, and the two World Wars at home: the munitions works and depots, the airmen and nurses, the bombs and the memorial.
  • On a plinth 9 places
    Who Nottingham has put on a plinth, in the order the statues and memorials went up: a Chartist, a regiment, an MP, a queen, an airman, the city's war dead, a hosier, an outlaw and a football manager.
  • Lost Nottingham 19 places
    Buildings and streets that are gone: pulled down for the Council House, the Victoria and Broad Marsh centres and Maid Marian Way, or burned and closed. Their pins mark where they stood.
  • Great houses 29 places
    The halls of the families who owned the land round Nottingham, and the villages and estates they shaped.
  • Faith 45 places
    Churches and chapels from the medieval parish churches to the Methodist missions, and the preachers who founded movements here.
  • Women's lives 24 places
    Nottingham women who campaigned for the vote, ran schools, businesses and libraries, healed, built and wrote.
  • Writers and artists 29 places
    The poets, novelists, painters and photographers who lived, worked or were born here.
  • Sport 27 places
    Cricket at Trent Bridge, football on both banks of the Trent, prize-fighters, skaters and rowers.
